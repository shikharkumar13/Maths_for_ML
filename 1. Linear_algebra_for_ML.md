## Introduction: Why Linear Algebra Is the Grammar of Machine Learning

Here is a confession most textbooks will not make: linear algebra sounds intimidating almost entirely because of its name. Strip away the Latin and what you are left with is a remarkably practical toolkit for answering one question — *how do you describe things with numbers, and how do you reason over those descriptions at massive scale?*

Think about how you would describe a friend to someone who has never met them. You might say: tall, early thirties, works in finance, prefers action films, drinks too much coffee. That description is already a vector — an ordered list of attributes. Now imagine doing the same for every customer of a bank, every song on a streaming platform, every word in a language. You need a mathematical system designed precisely for that: storing, comparing, and transforming collections of numbers efficiently. That system is **linear algebra**.

Before a machine learning model can recognise a face, recommend a product, or detect fraud, it must first transform the world into numbers — and then reason over those numbers at massive scale. Every row of a dataset is a vector. Every neural network layer is a matrix transformation. Every recommendation engine is performing vector similarity calculations thousands of times per second.

This article walks through four foundational pillars of linear algebra — introduction to the field, vectors and their operations, matrices and their operations, and matrix multiplication with dot products — and ties each concept back to practical, business-relevant machine learning use cases. Every concept is accompanied by runnable Python code and real-world analogies so you can build genuine intuition, not just mechanical familiarity.

## Section 1: Introduction to Linear Algebra

### What Linear Algebra Actually Is

Linear algebra studies **linear transformations** — functions that preserve addition and scalar multiplication. In practical terms, it provides tools to:

- Represent structured data as ordered collections of numbers
- Transform data from one representation into another
- Measure relationships and similarities between data points
- Solve systems of equations efficiently, even at massive scale

At its core, linear algebra gives us a language to talk about *direction*, *magnitude*, and *transformation* in high-dimensional spaces.

### The Business Problem It Solves

> **🏦 Story: The Bank That Couldn't Compare Customers**
>
> Imagine you run a bank in 1990 and you need to decide whether to approve a loan. You have a folder on your desk with every applicant's information: age, income, years employed, existing debt, credit history. A good loan officer can eyeball two folders and develop a gut feel for which applicant is a safer bet.
>
> Now imagine it is 2024 and your bank receives 50,000 loan applications per day across digital channels. No amount of gut feel scales to that. You need a way to represent each applicant as something a computer can compare mathematically — instantly, consistently, and at scale.
>
> Linear algebra is the answer. You convert each applicant's attributes into an ordered list of numbers. You convert the bank's historical knowledge of good and bad borrowers into matrices of learned weights. You compare, rank, and decide — millions of times a day — using nothing but the arithmetic of vectors and matrices.

### Scalars, Vectors, and Matrices: The Three Layers

Before anything else, let us build a mental model using something everyone has used: a **hospital**.

- When a nurse records your temperature as **37.2°C**, that single number is a **scalar**. One measurement, one dimension.
- When a doctor orders a full blood panel — haemoglobin, white cell count, platelets, glucose, creatinine — and gets back a list of six results, that list is a **vector**. One patient, many measurements.
- When a hospital stores the blood panel results for every patient admitted this month, that table — patients as rows, measurements as columns — is a **matrix**. Many patients, many measurements.

Every machine learning dataset lives at this third level: a matrix where each row is one example and each column is one feature.

```python
import numpy as np

# ── Scalar: a single number ───────────────────────────────────────────────────
temperature = 37.2                    # one patient, one measurement
print(f"Scalar : {temperature}  |  type: {type(temperature).__name__}")

# ── Vector: one patient's full blood panel ────────────────────────────────────
blood_panel = np.array([14.2, 7.8, 250.0, 95.0, 0.9])
#                       Hb    WBC  Platelets Glucose Creatinine
print(f"Vector : {blood_panel}  |  shape: {blood_panel.shape}")

# ── Matrix: all patients this month ───────────────────────────────────────────
patient_records = np.array([
    [14.2, 7.8, 250.0, 95.0, 0.9],   # Patient 1
    [11.5, 9.1, 180.0, 110.0, 1.2],  # Patient 2
    [13.8, 6.5, 320.0, 88.0, 0.8],   # Patient 3
])
print(f"Matrix shape: {patient_records.shape}  →  {patient_records.shape[0]} patients × {patient_records.shape[1]} measurements")
```

## Section 2: Vectors and Vector Operations

### What Is a Vector?

A **vector** is an ordered list of numbers that encodes a point or direction in space. Formally, an n-dimensional vector **v** is written as:

```
v = [v₁, v₂, v₃, ..., vₙ]
```

Each element `vᵢ` is a component, and `n` is the dimensionality of the vector.

> **🎵 Analogy: Your Music Taste as a Vector**
>
> Imagine rating your music preferences on a scale of 0 to 10 across four genres: Pop, Rock, Jazz, Classical. If you score them as `[8, 5, 2, 1]`, you have just described yourself as a vector in 4-dimensional "taste space." A friend who scores `[7, 6, 3, 1]` has a very similar vector — and a recommendation algorithm would immediately notice you two have similar taste and might enjoy the same playlists. A stranger who scores `[1, 2, 9, 8]` is pointing in a completely different direction in that space — and the algorithm would not recommend your playlists to them.
>
> This is literally how Spotify's recommendation engine works, except instead of 4 dimensions it uses hundreds, and instead of genre ratings it learns abstract musical features from listening history.

```python
# ── Customer profile vector ───────────────────────────────────────────────────
customer_A = np.array([34, 75_000, 720, 3])   # age, income, credit_score, products
customer_B = np.array([28, 52_000, 680, 1])

# ── Word embedding (simplified 4-D; real models use 768–4096 dims) ────────────
king  = np.array([ 0.92,  0.15, -0.37,  0.81])
queen = np.array([ 0.89,  0.12, -0.40,  0.79])
man   = np.array([ 0.30,  0.72, -0.18,  0.10])

# ── MNIST: 28×28 image → 784-dim vector ──────────────────────────────────────
fake_image = np.random.randint(0, 256, (28, 28))
image_vec  = fake_image.flatten()
print(f"Image shape : {fake_image.shape}")
print(f"As vector   : {image_vec.shape}  (each pixel becomes one dimension)")
```

### Core Vector Operations

#### 1. Vector Addition and Subtraction

> **🗺️ Analogy: Walking Through a City**
>
> Imagine you are navigating a city on foot. You walk 3 blocks east and 1 block north — that journey is a vector `[3, 1]`. Then you continue 1 block east and 3 blocks north — another vector `[1, 3]`. Where did you end up? Exactly `[3+1, 1+3] = [4, 4]`. You just did vector addition.
>
> Vector subtraction works the same way in reverse: if you know where you started and where you ended up, subtraction tells you the displacement — the gap between two data points. In ML, this gap is precisely what the error signal measures — how far the model's prediction is from the truth.

Vectors of the same dimension can be added or subtracted element-wise:

```python
a = np.array([1, 2, 3])
b = np.array([4, 5, 6])

print(f"a + b = {a + b}")   # [5 7 9]
print(f"a - b = {a - b}")   # [-3 -3 -3]

# ── K-Means centroid update ───────────────────────────────────────────────────
# Finding the "average customer" in a segment is just summing their vectors
# and dividing by the count — this is the centroid update step in K-Means.
customers = np.array([
    [0.20, 0.80, 0.60],   # Customer 1: young, high usage, good credit
    [0.30, 0.70, 0.50],   # Customer 2: similar profile
    [0.90, 0.10, 0.20],   # Customer 3: very different — older, low usage
    [0.80, 0.20, 0.30],
    [0.25, 0.75, 0.55],
])
centroid = customers.mean(axis=0)
print(f"\nCluster centroid (the 'average' customer): {centroid.round(3)}")
```

#### 2. Scalar Multiplication

> **🔊 Analogy: The Volume Knob**
>
> A scalar multiplied by a vector is exactly like a volume knob on a stereo. The knob does not change the *shape* of the music — the instruments, melody, and rhythm are all preserved — it only changes the *intensity*. Turn it to 2 and everything doubles. Turn it to 0.5 and everything halves. Turn it to -1 and the signal inverts.
>
> In gradient descent, the learning rate is the volume knob on the gradient signal. The gradient tells you *which direction* to adjust your model weights. The learning rate controls *how big* that adjustment is. Too loud (learning rate too high) and the model overshoots. Too quiet (learning rate too low) and learning crawls.

```python
learning_rate = 0.01                              # the "volume knob"
gradient      = np.array([3.2, -1.4, 0.8, 2.1])  # the "direction signal" from backprop

weight_update = learning_rate * gradient
print(f"Gradient      : {gradient}")
print(f"Weight update : {weight_update}")   # same direction, much smaller magnitude
```

#### 3. The Dot Product

The dot product is one of the most important operations in all of machine learning.

> **🎬 Analogy: How Much Will You Enjoy This Film?**
>
> Suppose you have rated how much you care about four aspects of a movie: Action, Dialogue, Visuals, and Humour. Your preference vector is `[8, 3, 7, 2]` (you love action and visuals, care less about dialogue and humour).
>
> A film critic describes a new movie along those same four axes: `[9, 2, 8, 1]` — lots of action and stunning visuals, minimal dialogue and humour.
>
> Your predicted enjoyment score is the **dot product**: `(8×9) + (3×2) + (7×8) + (2×1) = 72 + 6 + 56 + 2 = 136`. High score — you will probably love it.
>
> Now consider a rom-com rated `[1, 8, 3, 9]`. Dot product: `(8×1) + (3×8) + (7×3) + (2×9) = 8 + 24 + 21 + 18 = 71`. Lower score — not really your genre.
>
> This is the dot product in action: it measures *how aligned* two vectors are. In linear regression, the dot product of the weight vector and the feature vector *is* the prediction. In transformers, the dot product between query and key vectors determines attention weights. It is the universal measure of alignment in machine learning.

```python
a = np.array([1, 2, 3])
b = np.array([4, 5, 6])

# All three are equivalent — use whichever reads most clearly
print(f"sum-of-products : {sum(ai*bi for ai,bi in zip(a,b))}")
print(f"np.dot          : {np.dot(a, b)}")
print(f"@ operator      : {a @ b}")             # most Pythonic

# ── Linear regression prediction IS a dot product ────────────────────────────
weights  = np.array([0.3,   0.0001, 0.02, 0.5])   # learned weights (one per feature)
customer = np.array([34,   75_000,  720,  3  ])    # customer's feature values
bias     = -12.0
score    = weights @ customer + bias               # weighted sum = prediction
print(f"\nLoan approval score: {score:.2f}")
```

#### 4. Vector Norm (Magnitude)

> **📍 Analogy: How Far Did You Walk?**
>
> After your city walk of `[3, 4]` (3 blocks east, 4 blocks north), you might want to know how far you are from where you started — not the total steps taken (which would be 3 + 4 = 7, the L1 norm), but the straight-line distance as the crow flies (which is √(9 + 16) = 5, the L2 norm).
>
> In ML, the L2 norm of a weight vector is a measure of the model's "aggressiveness" — how far its parameters have strayed from zero. Ridge regression penalises this, essentially saying: "I want a model that gets the right answer, but doesn't wander too far from simple." That penalty is just the squared L2 norm of the weight vector, scaled by a regularisation constant.

```python
v = np.array([3.0, 4.0])

l1 = np.linalg.norm(v, ord=1)   # 7.0  — Manhattan (total blocks walked)
l2 = np.linalg.norm(v)          # 5.0  — Euclidean (straight-line distance)
print(f"L1 norm (total steps)       : {l1}")
print(f"L2 norm (straight-line dist): {l2}")

# ── Ridge regression penalty term ─────────────────────────────────────────────
weights_model = np.array([2.5, -1.8, 0.3, 4.1])
lambda_reg    = 0.01
l2_penalty    = lambda_reg * np.sum(weights_model**2)
print(f"\nL2 regularisation penalty : {l2_penalty:.4f}")
print("(This is added to the loss to discourage very large weights)")
```

#### 5. Cosine Similarity

> **🧭 Analogy: Two Hikers, Same Compass Direction**
>
> Imagine two hikers setting off from the same spot on a trail. One walks 2 km north-east. The other walks 10 km north-east. They have walked very different *distances* — but they are heading in exactly the *same direction*. Their compass bearings are identical.
>
> This is what cosine similarity captures: **the angle between two vectors, not their magnitudes**. If you and a friend both love the same genres of music but you have listened to 1,000 songs and they have listened to 10,000, your taste vectors point in the same direction — high cosine similarity — even though one vector is "larger."
>
> This matters enormously in NLP. The word "enormous" and the word "huge" appear very different numbers of times in any corpus, but their *meaning vectors* in a trained embedding model point in nearly the same direction. Cosine similarity finds this meaning-alignment.

```python
def cosine_similarity(a, b):
    """Angle between two vectors — ignores magnitude, measures direction alignment."""
    return np.dot(a, b) / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b))

# Word embeddings: king and queen are directionally close
print(f"king ↔ queen : {cosine_similarity(king, queen):.4f}")   # very close to 1
print(f"king ↔ man   : {cosine_similarity(king, man):.4f}")     # less aligned

# ── Recommendation system ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
# User's taste vector and item attribute vectors in the same space
user_profile = np.array([0.9, 0.1, 0.8, 0.2])   # loves action and visuals
item_action  = np.array([0.95, 0.05, 0.85, 0.1])  # action-heavy film
item_romance = np.array([0.1,  0.95, 0.05, 0.9])  # romance-heavy film

print(f"\nUser ↔ Action movie  : {cosine_similarity(user_profile, item_action):.4f}  ← recommend this")
print(f"User ↔ Romance movie : {cosine_similarity(user_profile, item_romance):.4f}  ← skip this")
```

## Section 3: Matrices and Matrix Operations

### What Is a Matrix?

> **📊 Analogy: Your Spreadsheet Has a Superpower**
>
> A matrix is, at its most basic, a spreadsheet — rows and columns of numbers. You have almost certainly worked with one in Excel or Google Sheets. Every dataset you have ever seen — a CSV file, a database table, an exported report — is a matrix in disguise.
>
> But unlike a passive spreadsheet that just stores data, a matrix in linear algebra is an *active mathematical object*. You can multiply matrices together to transform data from one representation into another. You can take their inverse to "undo" a transformation. You can compute their determinant to check whether the data in them is redundant.
>
> The shift from "spreadsheet" to "matrix" is the shift from *storing* information to *reasoning* with it.

An m × n matrix has m rows and n columns:

```
A = [[a₁₁, a₁₂, a₁₃],
     [a₂₁, a₂₂, a₂₃]]
```

### Building Matrices in NumPy

```python
# ── Dataset matrix: 5 customers × 4 features ─────────────────────────────────
# Each row is one customer (a vector). Stack them and you get the matrix.
X = np.array([
    [34,  75_000, 720, 3],
    [28,  52_000, 680, 1],
    [45,  92_000, 780, 5],
    [23,  38_000, 620, 0],
    [52, 115_000, 810, 7],
], dtype=float)

print(f"Dataset X shape: {X.shape}  →  {X.shape[0]} customers × {X.shape[1]} features")

# ── Identity matrix: the "do nothing" transformation ─────────────────────────
# Multiplying any matrix by the identity matrix gives you the same matrix back.
# It plays the same role as multiplying a number by 1.
I = np.eye(4)
print(f"\nIdentity (4×4):\n{I}")

# ── Random weight matrix for a neural layer ────────────────────────────────────
W = np.random.randn(8, 4) * 0.1
print(f"\nWeight matrix W shape: {W.shape}  (8 output neurons, each connected to 4 inputs)")
```

### Core Matrix Operations

#### 1. Matrix Addition — Gradient Accumulation

> When training a neural network across multiple mini-batches before performing a weight update, you need to collect (accumulate) the gradient signals from each batch. Matrix addition is how you combine them — you simply add the gradient matrices element-by-element, the same way you would add two spreadsheets cell-by-cell.

```python
grad_batch1 = np.array([[0.3, -0.1], [0.2,  0.4]])
grad_batch2 = np.array([[0.1,  0.2], [0.1, -0.1]])

accumulated_grad = grad_batch1 + grad_batch2
print(f"Gradient from batch 1:\n{grad_batch1}")
print(f"Gradient from batch 2:\n{grad_batch2}")
print(f"Accumulated gradient:\n{accumulated_grad}")
```

#### 2. Matrix Transpose — Rotating Your Spreadsheet

> **📄 Analogy: Rotating a Printed Table**
>
> Imagine printing your customer data table on a sheet of paper — customers as rows, features as columns. Now physically rotate that printout 90 degrees clockwise and read off what you see. Rows have become columns and columns have become rows. That is the **transpose** operation, written as Aᵀ.
>
> In neural network backpropagation, the gradient of the error needs to travel *backward* through each layer. The forward pass used the weight matrix W to transform inputs into outputs. The backward pass needs to transform errors from output space back into input space — and it does this using Wᵀ, the transpose. If W has shape (output × input), then Wᵀ has shape (input × output), which is exactly what you need to map errors backward.

```python
A = np.array([[1, 2, 3], [4, 5, 6]])
print(f"A  shape {A.shape}:\n{A}")
print(f"\nAᵀ shape {A.T.shape}:\n{A.T}")
print("Notice: rows became columns and columns became rows.")

# ── Backprop: gradient flows back through transposed weight matrix ─────────────
W_layer = np.random.randn(8, 4)   # weight matrix: 8 outputs, 4 inputs
delta   = np.random.randn(8, 1)   # error signal arriving from the next layer
grad_in = W_layer.T @ delta       # (4, 1) — error propagated backward to inputs
print(f"\nForward  W shape        : {W_layer.shape}   (transforms 4 → 8)")
print(f"Backward W.T shape      : {W_layer.T.shape}   (transforms 8 → 4, going backward)")
print(f"grad_in shape           : {grad_in.shape}   (gradient w.r.t. the 4 inputs)")
```

#### 3. Element-wise (Hadamard) Product — Dropout

> **🎲 Analogy: The Random Exam Question Selector**
>
> Imagine a professor who, to prevent students from cramming only certain topics, randomly covers 50% of exam questions with sticky notes before each student sees the paper. Every student gets a different random subset uncovered. No one can rely on any single question being there.
>
> Neural network **dropout** works identically. Before each training step, a random "mask" matrix of 0s and 1s is generated — 0 means "cover this neuron," 1 means "leave it active." This mask is then element-wise multiplied with the activation matrix. The neurons that get zeroed out must not be relied upon, forcing the network to learn redundant, robust representations. The element-wise multiply that implements this is the Hadamard product.

```python
activations  = np.array([[1.5, -0.3,  2.1,  0.8,  1.2]])
dropout_rate = 0.5
mask = (np.random.rand(*activations.shape) > dropout_rate).astype(float)
dropped = activations * mask                         # Hadamard product
scaled  = dropped / (1 - dropout_rate)              # re-scale to preserve expected value

print(f"Activations    : {activations}")
print(f"Dropout mask   : {mask}   (1=active, 0=silenced)")
print(f"After dropout  : {scaled}")
print("Zeroed neurons cannot be 'memorised' — the network must generalise.")
```

#### 4. Determinant — Detecting Redundant Information

> **🔍 Analogy: Two Rulers That Measure the Same Thing**
>
> Suppose your customer dataset has two features: "Height in centimetres" and "Height in inches." These are not two independent pieces of information — one is just a scaled version of the other. If you tried to use both features in a linear model, the model would be confused: both columns are saying the exact same thing in different units. The **determinant** detects this.
>
> When features are perfectly redundant (one is a linear combination of others), the determinant of the feature matrix collapses to exactly zero. A determinant of zero is the mathematical equivalent of a flashing warning sign: "Your data has redundant columns. You cannot invert this matrix, and any model trained on it will be unstable."

```python
# Good matrix: features carry independent information
A_good = np.array([[4, 3], [2, 1]], dtype=float)
print(f"Independent features  →  det = {np.linalg.det(A_good):.2f}  ✓  (model is stable)")

# Singular matrix: row 2 = 2 × row 1 (height in cm vs height in inches)
A_bad = np.array([[1, 2], [2, 4]], dtype=float)
print(f"Redundant features    →  det = {np.linalg.det(A_bad):.4f}  ✗  (model will be unstable)")
print("\nA determinant near zero means: some features carry no new information.")
```

#### 5. Matrix Inverse — The Undo Button

> **↩️ Analogy: The Ctrl+Z of Linear Algebra**
>
> If a matrix A represents a transformation — say, rotating your data by 45 degrees and stretching it by a factor of 2 — then A⁻¹ (the inverse) is the operation that perfectly undoes it: rotate back by 45 degrees and shrink by a factor of 2. Apply A then A⁻¹ and you are exactly back where you started.
>
> The most famous use of the matrix inverse in machine learning is the **closed-form solution to linear regression**. Instead of iteratively searching for the best weights, you can compute them directly in one shot using the formula below. It is elegant and exact — but requires inverting a matrix, which becomes very expensive when you have thousands of features.

```python
np.random.seed(42)
n, p = 200, 3

X_raw = np.random.randn(n, p)
X_ols = np.hstack([np.ones((n, 1)), X_raw])    # add bias column (n, 4)
true_w = np.array([2.0, 1.5, -0.8, 3.2])
y = X_ols @ true_w + 0.5 * np.random.randn(n)

# ── Closed-form: w = (XᵀX)⁻¹ Xᵀy  ───────────────────────────────────────────
# In words: "The best weights are found by inverting the feature covariance
# matrix and projecting the labels onto the feature space."
w_ols = np.linalg.inv(X_ols.T @ X_ols) @ X_ols.T @ y
w_lstsq, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X_ols, y, rcond=None)   # numerically stable equivalent

print("True weights :", true_w)
print("OLS weights  :", w_ols.round(3))
print("lstsq weights:", w_lstsq.round(3))   # should match closely
print("\n(For large datasets with many features, gradient descent is preferred")
print(" over the inverse because inverting huge matrices is computationally expensive.)")
```

## Section 4: Matrix Multiplication and Dot Products

### The Rules of Matrix Multiplication

> **🏭 Analogy: The Factory Assembly Line**
>
> Imagine a factory with a team of workers (the rows of matrix A). Each worker has a different skill profile — Worker 1 is great at welding and mediocre at painting; Worker 2 is the opposite. Now imagine a set of tasks (the columns of matrix B), each requiring different amounts of welding, painting, and other skills.
>
> Matrix multiplication computes one thing: for each (worker, task) pair, how well does this worker's skill set match what this task requires? The answer — the dot product of that worker's skill row with that task's requirement column — fills one cell of the output matrix C.
>
> Every cell in C is one dot product. The entire matrix multiplication is a comprehensive fitness check of every worker against every task, computed simultaneously.
>
> Replace "workers" with "neurons" and "tasks" with "input samples," and you have the neural network forward pass.

For matrices **A** (m × k) and **B** (k × n):

- The **inner dimensions must match** (both k)
- The result **C = AB** has shape m × n
- Each element `cᵢⱼ` is the dot product of row `i` of A with column `j` of B
- Matrix multiplication is **not commutative** — AB ≠ BA in general

```python
A = np.array([[1, 2], [3, 4]])
B = np.array([[5, 6], [7, 8]])

C = A @ B
print(f"A @ B =\n{C}")
# [[19 22]
#  [43 50]]

# ── Dimension check for a neural layer ────────────────────────────────────────
# Think of this as: 128 neurons, each with 64 weighted connections,
# processing a batch of 32 customer profiles simultaneously.
W_layer = np.random.randn(128, 64)   # weight matrix
X_batch = np.random.randn(64, 32)    # batch of 32 samples, 64 features each
output  = W_layer @ X_batch          # (128, 32) — 128 activations per sample

print(f"\nW_layer : {W_layer.shape}  — 128 neurons, 64 inputs each")
print(f"X_batch : {X_batch.shape}  — 32 samples, 64 features each")
print(f"Output  : {output.shape}  — 128 activations for each of 32 samples")
print("One matrix multiply processes the entire batch at once.")
```

### Complete Neural Network Forward Pass

> **📞 Analogy: A Game of Telephone With Numbers**
>
> The forward pass through a neural network is like a game of telephone played with mathematics. Raw data (customer features, pixel values, word indices) enters one end. Each layer transforms the message — stretching, rotating, and non-linearly squashing it — until a final answer emerges at the other end. Each transformation step is one matrix multiplication followed by a non-linear activation function. Stack enough of these and the network can approximate any function.

```python
np.random.seed(0)

def relu(z):    return np.maximum(0, z)
def sigmoid(z): return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))

# Architecture: 4 customer features → 8 hidden neurons → 1 churn probability
W1 = np.random.randn(8, 4) * 0.1;  b1 = np.zeros((8, 1))
W2 = np.random.randn(1, 8) * 0.1;  b2 = np.zeros((1, 1))

x = np.array([[0.5], [0.75], [0.9], [0.2]])   # one customer's normalised features

# Step 1: Linear transform + non-linear activation (first layer)
z1    = W1 @ x + b1       # weighted sum for each of 8 neurons — pure linear algebra
a1    = relu(z1)           # non-linear squash — negative values become zero

# Step 2: Linear transform + sigmoid (output layer)
z2    = W2 @ a1 + b2      # weighted sum of 8 hidden activations
y_hat = sigmoid(z2)        # convert to probability between 0 and 1

print(f"z1 shape : {z1.shape}  (8 neurons activated by 4 inputs)")
print(f"a1 shape : {a1.shape}  (after ReLU — same shape, some values zeroed)")
print(f"y_hat    : {y_hat[0,0]:.4f}  (churn probability — one number out of all that algebra)")
```

### Transformer Scaled Dot-Product Attention

> **🏛️ Analogy: A Room Full of Experts Who Consult Each Other**
>
> When a language model reads the sentence *"The bank near the river flooded,"* it must figure out that "bank" means riverbank, not a financial institution. How? It lets every word consult every other word and ask: *"Given who you are, how should I interpret myself?"*
>
> Each word is encoded as three vectors — a **Query** (what am I looking for?), a **Key** (what do I offer?), and a **Value** (what information do I carry?).
>
> The dot product between a word's Query and every other word's Key tells us how relevant each consultation is. "Bank" asking "river" for help (high dot product) matters more than "bank" asking "the" for help (low dot product). These relevance scores become attention weights, and the word's final representation is a weighted sum of everyone's Values — a consensus built from the most relevant consultations.
>
> The entire mechanism is four matrix multiplications. That is it.

```python
def softmax(x, axis=-1):
    e = np.exp(x - x.max(axis=axis, keepdims=True))
    return e / e.sum(axis=axis, keepdims=True)

np.random.seed(1)
seq_len, d_k = 6, 8    # 6 tokens (words), 8-dimensional key/query vectors

Q = np.random.randn(seq_len, d_k)   # each word's "what am I looking for?"
K = np.random.randn(seq_len, d_k)   # each word's "what do I offer?"
V = np.random.randn(seq_len, d_k)   # each word's actual information content

# Step 1 — How relevant is each word to each other? (dot products)
scores  = Q @ K.T / np.sqrt(d_k)   # (6×6) relevance matrix

# Step 2 — Normalise relevance scores to probabilities (attention weights)
weights = softmax(scores)           # each row sums to 1

# Step 3 — Each word's new representation = weighted sum of all values
output  = weights @ V               # (6×8) — updated word representations

print(f"Scores (6×6)  : {scores.shape}   — how much each word attends to every other")
print(f"Weights (6×6) : {weights.shape}  — softmax-normalised attention")
print(f"Output (6×8)  : {output.shape}   — contextualised word representations")
```

### Search Engine Relevance in One Matrix Multiply

> **📚 Analogy: The Library Card Catalogue, Reimagined**
>
> In an old library, finding a relevant book meant manually scanning a card catalogue — slow, sequential, exhausting. A modern search engine does something far more elegant: it encodes your query as a vector and every document in its index as a vector. Finding the most relevant results then becomes a single matrix multiplication — your query vector multiplied against the entire document matrix — producing a relevance score for every document simultaneously, in one operation.
>
> What used to take a librarian hours now takes a GPU milliseconds. The only thing that changed is we started representing knowledge as vectors.

```python
np.random.seed(7)
n_docs, dim = 1_000, 64

D = np.random.randn(n_docs, dim)
D /= np.linalg.norm(D, axis=1, keepdims=True)   # unit-normalise (pure direction)

q = np.random.randn(dim)
q /= np.linalg.norm(q)                          # unit-normalise query

# ONE matrix-vector multiply scores ALL 1,000 documents against the query
scores_s = D @ q                                 # shape: (1000,)
top5     = np.argsort(scores_s)[::-1][:5]

print("Top-5 documents by relevance (found in one matrix multiply):")
for rank, idx in enumerate(top5, 1):
    print(f"  #{rank}  doc_{idx:04d}  cosine_score={scores_s[idx]:.4f}")
```

## Bringing It Together: End-to-End Churn Prediction Pipeline

> **🔬 Story: From Customer Records to Predictions in Pure Linear Algebra**
>
> A telecom company wants to predict which customers are likely to cancel their subscriptions next month. They have a database of 300 customers, each described by four attributes: how long they have been a customer (Tenure), how much they spend monthly (MonthlySpend), how many times they have called support (SupportCalls), and the value tier of their plan (PlanValue).
>
> Every step of the predictive pipeline — from raw data ingestion to the final accuracy score — is a linear algebra operation. There is no magic. There are no black boxes. Just vectors and matrices, multiplied and inverted in the right order.

```python
np.random.seed(42)
n = 300
feature_names = ['Tenure', 'MonthlySpend', 'SupportCalls', 'PlanValue']

# ── Step 1: Raw data is a matrix (rows = customers, columns = features) ───────
X_raw  = np.random.randn(n, 4)
true_w = np.array([0.5, -0.3, 0.8, -0.2])
y = (X_raw @ true_w > 0).astype(float)   # 1 = churned, 0 = stayed

# ── Step 2: Normalise using vector norms (mean and std of each feature) ────────
mean, std = X_raw.mean(0), X_raw.std(0)
X_norm = (X_raw - mean) / std    # every feature now has mean 0 and std 1

# ── Step 3: Add a bias column of ones (the intercept term) ────────────────────
X_b = np.hstack([np.ones((n, 1)), X_norm])    # shape: (300, 5)

# ── Step 4: OLS closed-form solution (matrix inverse + multiply) ──────────────
w = np.linalg.lstsq(X_b, y, rcond=None)[0]

# ── Step 5: Predictions are just a matrix multiply ────────────────────────────
y_pred = (X_b @ w > 0.5).astype(float)

# ── Step 6: Evaluate ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
acc = (y_pred == y).mean()
print(f"Pipeline accuracy : {acc:.1%}")

# ── Step 7: Feature importance — absolute weight magnitude ────────────────────
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(9, 4))
labels      = ['Bias'] + feature_names
importances = np.abs(w)
colors      = [COLORS[i % len(COLORS)] for i in range(len(labels))]
bars = ax.barh(labels, importances, color=colors, edgecolor='white', linewidth=0.5)
ax.set_xlabel('|Weight| — Feature Importance\n(larger = more influential on churn)')
ax.set_title('What the OLS Solution Learned About Churn\n'
             '(SupportCalls weight is largest → most predictive of churn)', color='white')
for bar, val in zip(bars, importances):
    ax.text(val + 0.002, bar.get_y() + bar.get_height()/2,
            f'{val:.3f}', va='center', fontsize=9)
ax.invert_yaxis()
plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('07_feature_importance.png', dpi=150, bbox_inches='tight', facecolor='#0f1117')
plt.show()
```
