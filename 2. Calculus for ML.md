## Introduction: How Machines Learn to Get Better

Think about the first time you learned to ride a bicycle. You climbed on, wobbled, probably fell. You got back up, adjusted your balance slightly — leaning less to the left, gripping the handlebars differently — and tried again. Each attempt gave you information about what went wrong. Each adjustment made the next attempt fractionally better. After dozens of iterations, something clicked: you were riding.

This process — make an attempt, measure how badly you failed, identify which adjustments would help, apply them, repeat — is learning. And it turns out to be the exact same process by which every machine learning model trains, described precisely in the language of **calculus**.

In the previous article, we established that linear algebra gives machine learning systems the ability to *represent* data and *compute* with it. But representation alone does not make a system intelligent. Intelligence requires the ability to learn — to look at errors, understand their source, and adjust behaviour to do better next time. That capacity is powered by **differential calculus**.

Specifically, differential calculus gives us tools to measure how sensitive an output is to changes in its inputs. In machine learning, this translates to a fundamental question: *if I adjust this model parameter slightly, how does the prediction error change?* Answer that question for every parameter simultaneously, and you have a recipe for automated learning — a recipe called **gradient descent**.

This article covers four pillars: an introduction to calculus for ML, derivatives and their applications, the chain rule and partial derivatives, and gradients and gradient descent. Every concept is grounded in an intuitive analogy before the mathematics is introduced, and every mathematical idea is accompanied by runnable Python code.

## Section 1: Introduction to Calculus for Machine Learning

### The Central Problem: Minimising Loss

> **🏹 Analogy: Archery Practice in the Dark**
>
> Imagine you are learning archery in a completely dark room. You cannot see the target, but after each shot, an assistant calls out a single number: the distance in centimetres between where your arrow landed and the bullseye. That number is your **loss** — how wrong you were.
>
> Your goal is to find the stance, grip, and angle that consistently produces the smallest possible number. You cannot try every possible combination of arm position and release angle — there are too many. What you *can* do is make a shot, observe how the number changed from last time, reason about which adjustment might bring it down, and try again.
>
> This is precisely what a machine learning model does during training. The "stance and grip" are the model's parameters (weights). The "distance from bullseye" is the loss function. And the reasoning about which adjustment to make next — that is calculus.

Every supervised machine learning model is, at its heart, an optimisation problem. We define a **loss function** that measures how wrong the model's predictions are. Training means finding the parameter values that make this loss as small as possible:

```
Goal: find parameters θ* such that θ* = argmin L(θ)
```

```python
import numpy as np

# Simulate a simple 1-D loss landscape: L(w) = (w - 3)² + 1
# Think of w as a single model weight; L(w) as the prediction error.
w_range = np.linspace(-2, 8, 400)
L = (w_range - 3)**2 + 1

w_opt = 3.0
L_opt = (w_opt - 3)**2 + 1

print(f"Loss at w=0   : {(0-3)**2+1:.2f}              ← weight too low")
print(f"Loss at w=6   : {(6-3)**2+1:.2f}              ← weight too high")
```

### Three Types of Optima

> **⚽ Analogy: A Marble on a Hilly Football Pitch at Night**
>
> Imagine rolling a marble across a football pitch in complete darkness. You cannot see the terrain — only feel where the marble rolls. If the pitch has many dips and hills, the marble might roll into a small hollow near the corner flag and stop there, never reaching the deeper central depression that is the true lowest point of the field.
>
> That corner hollow is a **local minimum** — lower than its immediate surroundings but not the lowest point on the whole pitch. The deepest central depression is the **global minimum**. And there might be a ridge where the pitch slopes downhill in one direction but uphill in another — that is a **saddle point**.
>
> Real ML loss surfaces are extremely high-dimensional versions of this terrain. Gradient descent, like our marble, can only feel the local slope — and can get trapped. Techniques like momentum (the marble builds up speed), random restarts, and large batch sizes help escape these traps.

## Section 2: Derivatives and Their Applications

### What Is a Derivative?

> **🚗 Analogy: The Speedometer of a Function**
>
> Imagine you are driving along a road. Your GPS tracks your total distance travelled over time — that is your function `f(t)`. But your speedometer shows something different: your speed *right now*, at this exact instant. It does not care how far you have driven in the last hour or how far you will drive in the next — it tells you only about this precise moment. That is the **derivative**.
>
> Formally, a derivative is the instantaneous rate of change of a function at a specific point. Just as your speedometer at any given second equals `(distance_now - distance_a_moment_ago) / (moment in time)`, the derivative of `f(x)` at a point `x` is the limit of that exact ratio as the time interval shrinks to zero.
>
> For a loss function, the derivative at a given weight value is its "speedometer reading": how fast is the loss changing as I nudge this weight? A large positive derivative means increasing the weight makes the loss shoot up quickly. A derivative of zero means we have found a flat region — possibly a minimum.

Formally:

```
f'(x) = lim[h→0] (f(x+h) - f(x-h)) / (2h)
```

```python
# Numerical derivative: approximate the speedometer reading with a tiny time window
def numerical_derivative(f, x, h=1e-5):
    return (f(x + h) - f(x - h)) / (2 * h)   # central difference — most accurate

f = lambda x: x**2    # our function: f(x) = x²,  f'(x) = 2x analytically

# Verify numerically at three points
for x0, expected in [(-2, -4), (0, 0), (3, 6)]:
    numerical = numerical_derivative(f, x0)
    print(f"f'({x0:+d}) = {numerical:.4f}   expected {expected}   "
          f"  interpretation: {'slope is 0, possible minimum' if expected==0 else ('loss rising steeply' if expected>0 else 'loss falling steeply')}")
```

**Output:**
```
f'(-2) = -4.0000   expected -4    interpretation: loss falling steeply
f'( 0) =  0.0000   expected  0    interpretation: slope is 0, possible minimum
f'(+3) =  6.0000   expected  6    interpretation: loss rising steeply
```

### Common Activation Functions and Their Derivatives

Derivatives of activation functions are the secret ingredient of backpropagation. During the backward pass, every neuron needs to pass the error signal through its own derivative — its local "speedometer" — before sending it further back.

```python
def d_relu(x):    return (x > 0).astype(float)             # 0 or 1 — no squishing
```

### Loss Function Derivatives

The derivative of a loss function is the feedback signal that tells the model exactly *how* to adjust each weight. Without it, the model would have no idea whether to increase or decrease a parameter.

```python
# ── MSE loss: used for regression problems ────────────────────────────────────
np.random.seed(42)
n = 50
X_data = np.linspace(0, 5, n)
y_true = 2.5 * X_data + 1.0 + np.random.randn(n)

def mse_loss(w, b):
    y_pred = w * X_data + b
    return np.mean((y_true - y_pred)**2)

def mse_gradients(w, b):
    y_pred    = w * X_data + b
    residuals = y_pred - y_true
    dL_dw = 2 * np.mean(residuals * X_data)   # ∂L/∂w
    dL_db = 2 * np.mean(residuals)             # ∂L/∂b
    return dL_dw, dL_db

w0, b0  = 0.0, 0.0
loss0   = mse_loss(w0, b0)
gw, gb  = mse_gradients(w0, b0)

print(f"Initial MSE loss       : {loss0:.4f}")
print(f"Gradient w.r.t. w (gw) : {gw:.4f}  ← positive means: increase w reduces loss")
print(f"Gradient w.r.t. b (gb) : {gb:.4f}  ← positive means: increase b reduces loss")
print("The gradient tells the model exactly where to move — this is learning.")

# ── Binary cross-entropy: used for classification ─────────────────────────────
def bce_loss(y_true, y_pred, eps=1e-9):
    return -np.mean(y_true * np.log(y_pred + eps) +
                    (1 - y_true) * np.log(1 - y_pred + eps))

yt = np.array([1, 0, 1, 1, 0])           # true labels
yp = np.array([0.9, 0.1, 0.8, 0.6, 0.3]) # predicted probabilities (quite good)
print(f"\nBCE loss (good predictions) : {bce_loss(yt, yp):.4f}")

yp_bad = np.array([0.1, 0.9, 0.2, 0.3, 0.8])  # predicted probabilities (bad)
print(f"BCE loss (bad predictions)  : {bce_loss(yt, yp_bad):.4f}  ← much higher")
```

### The Learning Rate — How Big a Step to Take

> **🧂 Analogy: Seasoning a Dish**
>
> Imagine you are cooking a soup and it needs more salt. You can feel it is under-seasoned — your "loss" is high. But how much salt do you add? If you tip the entire shaker in at once (huge learning rate), you will ruin the soup irreparably. If you add one grain at a time (tiny learning rate), you will be there until midnight. The right approach is to add a small, measured pinch, taste, assess, add another pinch if needed — converging to perfect seasoning through informed iteration.
>
> The **learning rate** in gradient descent is exactly this pinch size. The derivative tells you *which direction* to adjust (add salt vs. remove salt is not possible, but increase vs. decrease a weight is). The learning rate controls *how much* you adjust. Choose wisely — it is one of the most consequential decisions in training a model.

```python
# 1-D gradient descent on L(w) = (w-3)²
def loss_fn(w): return (w - 3)**2 + 1
def grad_fn(w): return 2 * (w - 3)

def gradient_descent_1d(lr, n_steps=25, w_init=0.0):
    w, history = w_init, [w_init]
    for _ in range(n_steps):
        w = w - lr * grad_fn(w)   # the core update rule
        history.append(w)
    return np.array(history)

w_vals = np.linspace(-2, 8, 400)
L_vals = loss_fn(w_vals)

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(15, 5))
fig.suptitle("The Learning Rate: Too Small, Just Right, Too Large\n"
             "(like adding salt: a grain, a pinch, or the whole shaker)",
             fontsize=12, fontweight='bold', color='white')

for ax, (lr, title) in zip(axes, [
    (0.01, 'lr=0.01  Too Small\n→ Converges, but crawls'),
    (0.5,  'lr=0.50  Just Right\n→ Smooth, fast convergence'),
    (1.20, 'lr=1.20  Too Large\n→ Overshoots, diverges!'),
]):
    ax.plot(w_vals, L_vals, color=COLORS[0], lw=2, label='Loss L(w)')
    history = gradient_descent_1d(lr, n_steps=25)
    L_hist  = loss_fn(history)

    ax.scatter(history, L_hist, c=range(len(history)), cmap='cool', s=55, zorder=5)
    ax.plot(history, L_hist, color=COLORS[1], lw=1.5, alpha=0.6)
    ax.annotate('', xy=(history[1], L_hist[1]), xytext=(history[0], L_hist[0]),
                arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle='->', color='white', lw=1.5))

    ax.set_xlabel('w  (weight)'); ax.set_ylabel('L(w)  (loss)')
    ax.set_title(title, color='white', fontsize=9)
    ax.set_xlim(-2, 8); ax.set_ylim(0, 35)
    ax.legend(fontsize=8); ax.grid(True, alpha=0.3)

plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('12_learning_rate.png', dpi=150, bbox_inches='tight', facecolor='#0f1117')
plt.show()
```

### Second Derivatives and Curvature

> **🛣️ Analogy: Road Curvature**
>
> Your first derivative tells you the current slope of the road — are you going uphill or downhill? The second derivative tells you how that slope is *changing* — is the road bending upward (becoming steeper) or bending downward (levelling out)?
>
> If you are heading downhill on a road that is bending upward (second derivative > 0), you know a valley is ahead — a minimum. If you are heading downhill on a road that keeps getting steeper downward (second derivative < 0), you might be approaching a cliff — a maximum.
>
> Advanced ML optimisers like Newton's Method and L-BFGS use second derivative information (via the **Hessian matrix**) to take much smarter steps — like a driver who not only knows the current slope but can see the upcoming road curvature and plans their speed accordingly.

```python
# Second derivatives reveal curvature — is there a bowl or a hill here?
f   = lambda x:   x**3 - 3*x     # a function with both a minimum and a maximum
df  = lambda x:   3*x**2 - 3     # first derivative: f'(x) = 3x² - 3
ddf = lambda x:   6*x             # second derivative: f''(x) = 6x

x_range = np.linspace(-2.5, 2.5, 300)

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(15, 5))
fig.suptitle("f(x), f'(x), and f''(x) — Function, Slope, and Curvature",
             fontsize=12, fontweight='bold', color='white')

for ax, (fn, title, col) in zip(axes, [
    (f,   "f(x) = x³ − 3x\n(function)", COLORS[0]),
    (df,  "f'(x) = 3x² − 3\n(slope — zero at critical points)", COLORS[1]),
    (ddf, "f''(x) = 6x\n(curvature: +ve=bowl, −ve=hill)", COLORS[2]),
]):
    ax.plot(x_range, fn(x_range), color=col, lw=2.5)
    ax.axhline(0, color='white', lw=0.8, alpha=0.5)
    ax.axvline(0, color='white', lw=0.5, alpha=0.5)
    ax.fill_between(x_range, fn(x_range), alpha=0.1, color=col)

    # Mark where f'=0 (critical points: x=±1)
    for xc in [-1, 1]:
        ax.scatter([xc], [fn(xc)], color=COLORS[3], s=100, zorder=5)
        concavity = 'min' if ddf(xc) > 0 else 'max'
        ax.text(xc+0.05, fn(xc)+0.3, f'x={xc}\n({concavity})', color=COLORS[3], fontsize=7)

    ax.set_xlabel('x'); ax.set_title(title, color='white', fontsize=9)
    ax.grid(True, alpha=0.3)

plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('10b_second_derivative.png', dpi=150, bbox_inches='tight', facecolor='#0f1117')
```

## Section 3: The Chain Rule and Partial Derivatives

### The Chain Rule

> **🏭 Analogy: Tracing a Defect Back Through a Factory**
>
> Imagine a factory that produces bottled tomato sauce. The production line has three stages: (1) a tomato press that makes puree, (2) a seasoning blender that adds spices, and (3) a bottling station that packages the result. One day, the bottled sauce tastes terrible. A quality control inspector arrives and asks: "How much is the bottling station to blame? How much is the seasoning blender responsible? How much is the tomato press at fault?"
>
> She cannot ask the bottling station directly about the tomatoes — it only knows about the blended liquid it receives. So she works backward: she asks the bottling station how sensitive it is to the blend it receives. Then she asks the blender how sensitive it is to the puree it received. Multiply these together and you have the total sensitivity of the final product to the raw tomato quality.
>
> This backward tracing of sensitivity is precisely the **chain rule**: when a function is composed of several stages, the derivative of the whole chain is the product of derivatives at each stage.

The chain rule: if `y = f(g(x))`, then:

```
dy/dx = f'(g(x)) × g'(x)  =  (dy/du) × (du/dx)   where u = g(x)
```

```python
# Chain rule: y = (3x + 2)⁴
# Stage 1: u = 3x + 2           → du/dx = 3
# Stage 2: y = u⁴               → dy/du = 4u³
# Total:   dy/dx = 4u³ × 3 = 12(3x+2)³

def y_composed(x):  return (3*x + 2)**4
def dy_dx_analytic(x): return 12 * (3*x + 2)**3   # chain rule applied by hand
def dy_dx_numeric(x):  return numerical_derivative(y_composed, x)

print("Verifying chain rule (analytic vs numeric):")
for xp in [-1, 0, 1, 2]:
    a = dy_dx_analytic(xp)
    n = dy_dx_numeric(xp)
    print(f"  x={xp:+d}  analytic={a:10.2f}  numeric={n:10.2f}  ✓" if abs(a-n)<0.1 else "  ✗ mismatch")
```

### Backpropagation via the Chain Rule

> **🔍 Analogy: The Quality Control Inspector Working Backwards**
>
> Our factory inspector from above — now called **backpropagation** — starts at the worst outcome (the loss, the terrible sauce) and works backward through every stage of production, computing at each stage: "How responsible was *this* step for the final badness?" She multiplies sensitivities as she goes, because each stage's contribution depends on everything downstream of it.
>
> In a neural network: the loss is the terrible sauce, the output layer is the bottling station, the hidden layers are the blender and press, and the input weights are the raw tomatoes. Backprop computes — layer by layer, using the chain rule — exactly how much each weight contributed to the final error. These contributions become the gradients that drive the weight update.

```python
# Manual backprop through a 3-layer network — no frameworks, pure chain rule
np.random.seed(0)

def sigmoid(z):   return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))
def d_sigmoid(z): return sigmoid(z) * (1 - sigmoid(z))
def relu(z):      return np.maximum(0, z)
def d_relu(z):    return (z > 0).astype(float)

# Network weights: 3 inputs → 4 hidden → 2 hidden → 1 output
W1 = np.random.randn(4, 3)*0.1;  b1 = np.zeros((4, 1))
W2 = np.random.randn(2, 4)*0.1;  b2 = np.zeros((2, 1))
W3 = np.random.randn(1, 2)*0.1;  b3 = np.zeros((1, 1))

x = np.random.randn(3, 1)      # one input sample
y_target = np.array([[1.0]])   # true label

# ── FORWARD PASS (left to right through the factory) ─────────────────────────
z1 = W1 @ x  + b1;  a1 = relu(z1)      # press: raw input → puree
z2 = W2 @ a1 + b2;  a2 = relu(z2)      # blender: puree → seasoned mix
z3 = W3 @ a2 + b3;  a3 = sigmoid(z3)   # bottler: mix → probability (0-1)
loss = -(y_target * np.log(a3+1e-9) + (1-y_target)*np.log(1-a3+1e-9))

# ── BACKWARD PASS (right to left — the inspector traces blame back) ──────────
# At each layer: gradient = (error from above) × (local derivative)
dL_da3 = -(y_target/(a3+1e-9)) + (1-y_target)/(1-a3+1e-9)
dL_dz3 = dL_da3 * d_sigmoid(z3)           # chain rule at Layer 3 (bottler)
dL_dW3 = dL_dz3 @ a2.T;   dL_da2 = W3.T @ dL_dz3

dL_dz2 = dL_da2 * d_relu(z2)              # chain rule at Layer 2 (blender)
dL_dW2 = dL_dz2 @ a1.T;   dL_da1 = W2.T @ dL_dz2

dL_dz1 = dL_da1 * d_relu(z1)              # chain rule at Layer 1 (press)
dL_dW1 = dL_dz1 @ x.T

print(f"Loss            : {loss[0,0]:.4f}")
print(f"|gradient at W3| : {np.abs(dL_dW3).mean():.6f}  (bottler — closest to error)")
print(f"|gradient at W2| : {np.abs(dL_dW2).mean():.6f}  (blender — one step removed)")
print(f"|gradient at W1| : {np.abs(dL_dW1).mean():.6f}  (press   — furthest from error, smallest signal)")
print("\n← The signal weakens as we go further from the output. This is the vanishing gradient problem.")
```

### Visualising Gradient Flow: Vanishing Gradients

> **📠 Analogy: Photocopy of a Photocopy**
>
> Make a photocopy of a document. Now photocopy that copy. Then photocopy the copy of the copy. By the tenth generation, the text is blurry and nearly unreadable. Information degrades at each reproduction step.
>
> When sigmoid activations are stacked in deep networks, the same thing happens to gradient signals. Sigmoid's derivative has a maximum value of only 0.25 — meaning the error signal is reduced to at most 25% of its strength at each layer it passes through. After ten layers, the gradient reaching the first layer is at most `0.25¹⁰ ≈ 0.000001` — essentially zero. The first layers stop learning entirely.
>
> **ReLU** solves this because its derivative is simply 1 for positive inputs — like a perfect, lossless photocopy. The gradient passes through unchanged, keeping early layers in the learning loop.

```python
n_layers = 10
sigmoid_max_deriv = 0.25   # gradient shrinks by 75% each time
```

### Partial Derivatives

> **🏥 Analogy: Isolating One Cause in a Hospital**
>
> A hospital nutritionist is studying what affects a patient's blood glucose level. Two factors matter: daily rice intake (x) and exercise duration (y). The full relationship is complex: `glucose = f(x, y)`. But to give clear dietary advice, the nutritionist needs to isolate each factor: "If I hold exercise constant at 30 minutes per day and only vary rice intake, how does glucose change?" That sensitivity to rice *alone*, holding everything else fixed, is the **partial derivative ∂f/∂x**.
>
> In a neural network with millions of weights, each weight is one "ingredient." A partial derivative tells us: holding all other weights constant, how does the loss change if I nudge only this one weight? Computing this for every weight simultaneously gives us the gradient — the full picture of which ingredients need adjusting and by how much.

```python
# f(x, y) = 3x² + 2xy + y³
# ∂f/∂x  = 6x + 2y   (how f changes with x, holding y fixed)
# ∂f/∂y  = 2x + 3y²  (how f changes with y, holding x fixed)

def f_xy(x, y):   return 3*x**2 + 2*x*y + y**3

def partial_y(f, x, y, h=1e-5): return (f(x, y+h) - f(x, y-h)) / (2*h)

print("Partial derivatives — analytic vs numeric:")
```

## Section 4: Gradients and Gradient Descent

### What Is a Gradient?

> **🏔️ Analogy: Navigating a Foggy Mountain Blindfolded**
>
> You have been dropped onto a mountain range wearing a blindfold. Your goal is to reach the lowest valley. You cannot see the terrain — but you can feel the slope beneath your feet right where you are standing.
>
> If the ground tilts up to your left and down to your right, you know stepping right takes you downhill. If it tilts up in front and to the right but down behind and to the left, you step diagonally backward-left. At every point, you can feel exactly which direction descends most steeply — you just cannot see anything beyond your own feet.
>
> The **gradient** is the mathematical version of this "slope beneath your feet." It is a vector pointing in the direction of *steepest uphill ascent* from your current position. To descend, you step in the **opposite** direction: the negative gradient. That is gradient descent.

```python
# Loss surface: L(w₁, w₂) = w₁² + 2·w₂²  (a simple bowl, minimum at origin)
def loss_surface(w1, w2):  return w1**2 + 2*w2**2

def gradient_loss(w1, w2):
    """The gradient vector [∂L/∂w₁, ∂L/∂w₂] — points uphill."""
    return np.array([2*w1, 4*w2])

# Standing at position (3, 2)
w = np.array([3.0, 2.0])
g = gradient_loss(*w)

print(f"Position       : w = {w}")
print(f"Loss at w      : L = {loss_surface(*w):.2f}")
print(f"Gradient ∇L    : {g}   ← 'you are standing on slope that rises in this direction'")
print(f"Negative grad  : {-g}  ← 'step here to go downhill fastest'")
print()
print("Gradient descent update (lr=0.1):")
lr = 0.1
w_new = w - lr * g
print(f"  w_new = {w} - 0.1 × {g} = {w_new}")
print(f"  New loss: {loss_surface(*w_new):.4f}  (was {loss_surface(*w):.2f} — improving!)")
```

### Gradient Descent: The River Finding the Sea

> **🌊 Analogy: A River Finding the Sea**
>
> A river does not need a map to find the ocean. It does not compute the optimal path. At every point, water simply follows the steepest available downward slope. Across millions of small steps over thousands of years, this mindless local rule carves the most efficient path from mountain to sea.
>
> Gradient descent is the mathematical river. At every step, the model follows the steepest downward slope on the loss surface — the negative gradient. It does not know the shape of the whole surface. It only knows the local slope at its current position. Yet across thousands of small steps, this local rule reliably finds a valley where loss is low and predictions are good.

```python
def gradient_descent_2d(lr=0.1, n_steps=40, w_init=None):
    w = np.array([3.0, 2.0]) if w_init is None else np.array(w_init, dtype=float)
    history = [w.copy()]
    for _ in range(n_steps):
        g = gradient_loss(*w)
        w = w - lr * g      # step opposite to gradient — the river flows downhill
        history.append(w.copy())
    return np.array(history)

history = gradient_descent_2d(lr=0.1, n_steps=40)
print("Gradient Descent Path — like water carving downhill (first 8 steps):")
for i, w in enumerate(history[:8]):
    print(f"  Step {i:2d}: w=[{w[0]:+.4f}, {w[1]:+.4f}]  Loss={loss_surface(*w):.4f}"
          + ("  ← start" if i==0 else ""))
```

### Gradient Descent Variants

> **📚 Analogy: Three Students Preparing for an Exam**
>
> Three students have the same textbook and the same exam. They each use a different study strategy:
>
> - **Batch GD student** reads every single page of the entire textbook before writing a single note or updating their understanding. Their notes are thorough and stable — but they spend so long reading that they only review the material once before the exam.
>
> - **SGD student** reads one sentence, immediately writes a note, reads another sentence, immediately revises their notes. They are incredibly fast but the notes are erratic — sometimes a single ambiguous sentence sends them in the completely wrong direction.
>
> - **Mini-batch student** reads one chapter at a time (32 pages), then writes notes, then reads the next chapter. They are not as thorough as the full-book reader, not as fast as the sentence-by-sentence reader — but in practice they consistently outperform both.
>
> The mini-batch strategy is what virtually every production ML system uses. It strikes the right balance between the quality of the gradient estimate and the speed of iteration.

```python
np.random.seed(42)
n_data = 200
X_gd  = np.random.randn(n_data, 1)
y_gd  = 3 * X_gd.squeeze() + 1.5 + 0.5 * np.random.randn(n_data)

def compute_gradient(X, y, w, b):
    y_pred = X @ w + b
    err    = y_pred - y
    return 2 * X.T @ err / len(y), 2 * err.mean(), np.mean(err**2)

def train(X, y, lr=0.05, n_epochs=60, batch_size=None, shuffle=True):
    w, b, losses, n = np.array([0.0]), 0.0, [], len(y)
    for epoch in range(n_epochs):
        idx = np.random.permutation(n) if shuffle else np.arange(n)
        X_s, y_s = X[idx], y[idx]
        bs = batch_size or n
        epoch_loss = []
        for start in range(0, n, bs):
            dw, db, loss = compute_gradient(X_s[start:start+bs], y_s[start:start+bs], w, b)
            w -= lr * dw;  b -= lr * db
            epoch_loss.append(loss)
        losses.append(np.mean(epoch_loss))
    return losses

loss_batch = train(X_gd, y_gd, lr=0.05, n_epochs=60, batch_size=None)   # reads whole book
loss_sgd   = train(X_gd, y_gd, lr=0.01, n_epochs=60, batch_size=1)      # reads one sentence
loss_mini  = train(X_gd, y_gd, lr=0.05, n_epochs=60, batch_size=32)     # reads one chapter

        label='Batch GD    — stable but slow (read whole book first)')

        label='Mini-Batch  — balanced sweet spot (one chapter at a time)')
ax.set_xlabel('Epoch'); ax.set_ylabel('MSE Loss')

             color='white')

plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('16_gd_variants.png', dpi=150, bbox_inches='tight', facecolor='#0f1117')
plt.show()
```

### Advanced Optimisers: Momentum, RMSProp, and Adam

> **🪨 Momentum: The Boulder Rolling Downhill**
>
> Vanilla gradient descent takes a step, stops completely, re-evaluates the slope, and takes another step. It has no memory of where it has been. **Momentum** gives it a memory — like a boulder rolling downhill that builds up speed. Even if the slope flattens briefly, the boulder keeps rolling in the same direction due to accumulated velocity. This lets it power through flat plateaus and small local bumps without getting stuck.

> **📊 RMSProp: The Experienced Hiker Who Knows the Terrain**
>
> An experienced hiker adjusts their step size based on the terrain they have covered. On a reliable, consistent downhill stretch they stride confidently. On unpredictable, jagged ground they move more carefully. **RMSProp** does the same per parameter — parameters with consistently large gradients (reliable direction) get smaller updates; parameters with small or erratic gradients get larger relative updates. It adapts the learning rate to the individual history of each weight.

> **🧭 Adam: A GPS That Combines Speed, Direction, and Road History**
>
> **Adam** (Adaptive Moment Estimation) is the GPS of optimisers. It simultaneously tracks: (1) the direction you have been trending recently (first moment — like momentum), and (2) how variable that direction has been (second moment — like RMSProp). Together, these give each parameter a custom, adaptive step size informed by both its recent trajectory and its historical volatility. This is why Adam works well out of the box across a huge range of model architectures and datasets — and why it became the default optimiser in deep learning.

```python
def run_optimiser(optimiser_fn, n_steps=60, w_start=3.0):
    """Run optimiser on L(w) = w² starting from w_start (minimum at w=0)."""
    w, state, path = w_start, {}, [w_start]
    for t in range(1, n_steps + 1):
        grad = 2 * w   # derivative of w²
        w, state = optimiser_fn(w, grad, t, state)
        path.append(w)
    return np.array(path)

def vanilla_sgd(w, g, t, state, lr=0.05):
    return w - lr * g, state

def sgd_momentum(w, g, t, state, lr=0.1, beta=0.9):
    v = beta * state.get('v', 0.0) - lr * g   # velocity accumulates
    state['v'] = v
    return w + v, state

def rmsprop(w, g, t, state, lr=0.1, beta=0.99, eps=1e-8):
    s = beta * state.get('s', 0.0) + (1 - beta) * g**2
    state['s'] = s
    return w - lr * g / (np.sqrt(s) + eps), state  # adaptive step per param

def adam(w, g, t, state, lr=0.3, b1=0.9, b2=0.999, eps=1e-8):
    m = b1 * state.get('m', 0.0) + (1 - b1) * g       # first moment (direction)
    v = b2 * state.get('v', 0.0) + (1 - b2) * g**2    # second moment (volatility)
    m_hat = m / (1 - b1**t)    # bias correction (important early in training)
    v_hat = v / (1 - b2**t)
    state.update({'m': m, 'v': v})
    return w - lr * m_hat / (np.sqrt(v_hat) + eps), state  # smart adaptive step

paths = {
    'Vanilla SGD (slow, steady)':    run_optimiser(vanilla_sgd),
    'SGD + Momentum (boulder)':      run_optimiser(sgd_momentum),
    'RMSProp (adaptive terrain)':    run_optimiser(rmsprop),
    'Adam (GPS — best of all)':      run_optimiser(adam),
}

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(14, 5))
fig.suptitle("Optimiser Comparison on L(w) = w²  (goal: reach w=0 as fast as possible)\n"
             "Adam combines momentum + adaptive rates — fastest reliable convergence",
             fontsize=11, fontweight='bold', color='white')

for (name, path), col in zip(paths.items(), COLORS):
    axes[0].plot(path,    color=col, lw=2, label=name)
    axes[1].plot(path**2, color=col, lw=2, label=name)

for ax, ylabel, title in zip(axes,
    ['w  (parameter value)', 'L(w) = w²  (loss)'],
    ['Parameter Convergence\n(all start at w=3, goal is w=0)',
     'Loss Convergence\n(Adam reaches near-zero fastest)']):
    ax.axhline(0, color='white', lw=0.8, ls='--', alpha=0.5)
    ax.set_xlabel('Training Step'); ax.set_ylabel(ylabel)
    ax.set_title(title, color='white')
    ax.legend(fontsize=8); ax.grid(True, alpha=0.3)

plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('17_optimisers.png', dpi=150, bbox_inches='tight', facecolor='#0f1117')
plt.show()
```

### Learning Rate Scheduling

> **🏃 Analogy: A Marathon Runner's Training Plan**
>
> Elite marathon runners do not train at full race intensity on day one. Their programme follows a deliberate arc: a **warmup** phase of gentle runs to condition joints and build aerobic base, a **peak training** phase of high-intensity intervals, and a **taper** phase in the final weeks before race day where volume drops sharply so the body can recover and arrive at peak fitness.
>
> Learning rate scheduling follows the same logic. The **warmup** phase at the start of training uses a tiny learning rate — the model's weights are random and the gradients are unstable; aggressive steps would destroy early progress. The **peak** phase uses the full learning rate once gradients have stabilised. The **cosine decay** phase gradually reduces the learning rate as training matures, allowing fine-grained refinement of weights without overshooting near-optimal values.
>
> This schedule is used in GPT, BERT, LLaMA, and virtually every production-scale language model. The marathon runner metaphor is not just poetic — it captures why each phase exists.

```python
n_steps    = 200
steps      = np.arange(n_steps)
lr_max, lr_min, T_warmup = 0.1, 1e-4, 20

def step_decay(t, lr0=0.1, drop=0.5, freq=40):
    return lr0 * (drop ** (t // freq))

def cosine_annealing(t, T=n_steps):
    return lr_min + 0.5*(lr_max-lr_min)*(1 + np.cos(np.pi * t / T))

def warmup_cosine(t):
    if t < T_warmup:
        return lr_max * t / T_warmup          # build up gradually — warmup phase
    return lr_min + 0.5*(lr_max-lr_min)*(1 + np.cos(np.pi*(t-T_warmup)/(n_steps-T_warmup)))

schedules = {
    'Step Decay        (staircase drops)',  [step_decay(t) for t in steps],
    'Cosine Annealing  (smooth S-curve)',   [cosine_annealing(t) for t in steps],
    'Warmup + Cosine   (used in GPT/BERT/LLaMA)', [warmup_cosine(t) for t in steps],
    'Constant          (baseline)',          [0.05] * n_steps,
}

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(12, 5))
for (name, sched), col in zip(schedules.items(), COLORS):
    ax.plot(steps, sched, color=col, lw=2.5, label=name)

ax.axvspan(0, T_warmup, alpha=0.08, color=COLORS[1], label=f'Warmup phase (0–{T_warmup} steps)')
ax.axvline(T_warmup, color='white', lw=1, ls=':', alpha=0.6)
ax.text(T_warmup+2, lr_max*0.92, 'warmup\nends', color='white', fontsize=8)

ax.set_xlabel('Training Step')
ax.set_ylabel('Learning Rate α')
ax.set_title('Learning Rate Schedules — Like a Marathon Training Plan\n'
             '(warm up → peak → taper for optimal final performance)', color='white')
ax.legend(fontsize=8); ax.grid(True, alpha=0.3)
plt.tight_layout()
```

## Complete Training Loop: Logistic Regression from Scratch

> **📖 Story: Teaching a Bank to Predict Loan Default**
>
> A bank wants to predict which loan applicants are likely to default. They have 500 historical records, each with four features — credit score, debt-to-income ratio, employment length, and loan amount — and a label: did the customer default (1) or not (0)?
>
> They decide to train a logistic regression model from scratch, using nothing but calculus and NumPy. Here is how the mathematics of this article — loss functions, derivatives, chain rule, gradients, Adam — assembles into a working system, one epoch at a time:
>
> **Epoch 1:** The model starts with all weights at zero — it has no opinion. It makes predictions (all 50% probability), measures how wrong it is, computes the gradient of that error with respect to every weight (chain rule), and takes a small Adam-guided step in the direction that reduces the error. Loss is high. Accuracy is random.
>
> **Epoch 50:** Weights have been updated hundreds of times. The gradient signals have become smaller — the model is near a valley. Predictions are improving noticeably.
>
> **Epoch 150:** The model has converged. Gradients are near zero (we are at a minimum). The loss has dropped substantially and accuracy is good. The bank can now use this model to score new applicants in milliseconds.
>
> Every line of the training loop below maps to a concept from this article.

```python
np.random.seed(42)

# ── Synthetic loan default dataset ────────────────────────────────────────────
n_train = 500
X_lr   = np.random.randn(n_train, 4)          # credit score, DTI, employment, loan amount
true_w = np.array([1.2, -0.8, 0.5, -1.5])     # the true pattern (hidden from model)
y_lr   = (X_lr @ true_w > np.random.randn(n_train) * 0.5).astype(float)

# Normalise (mean=0, std=1) and add bias column
X_lr   = (X_lr - X_lr.mean(0)) / X_lr.std(0)
X_b    = np.hstack([np.ones((n_train, 1)), X_lr])   # shape: (500, 5)

def sigmoid(z): return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-np.clip(z, -500, 500)))

def train_logistic(X, y, lr=0.05, n_epochs=150, batch_size=64):
    n, d = X.shape
    w = np.zeros(d)                # start with zero opinions
    losses, accs = [], []
    m_adam = np.zeros(d)           # Adam: first moment  (momentum)
    v_adam = np.zeros(d)           # Adam: second moment (adaptive scale)
    b1, b2, eps = 0.9, 0.999, 1e-8

    for epoch in range(n_epochs):
        idx = np.random.permutation(n)   # shuffle — mini-batch strategy
        epoch_loss = []

        for t_local, start in enumerate(range(0, n, batch_size), 1):
            Xb = X[idx[start:start+batch_size]]
            yb = y[idx[start:start+batch_size]]

            # ── FORWARD: make predictions (linear algebra) ────────────────────
            y_pred = sigmoid(Xb @ w)

            # ── LOSS: how wrong are we? (calculus — BCE) ─────────────────────
            loss = -np.mean(yb*np.log(y_pred+1e-9) + (1-yb)*np.log(1-y_pred+1e-9))
            epoch_loss.append(loss)

            # ── BACKWARD: chain rule → gradient of loss w.r.t. all weights ──
            g = Xb.T @ (y_pred - yb) / len(yb)   # ∂L/∂w via chain rule

            # ── UPDATE: Adam step (momentum + adaptive rate) ──────────────────
            t_global = epoch * (n // batch_size) + t_local
            m_adam   = b1 * m_adam + (1 - b1) * g
            v_adam   = b2 * v_adam + (1 - b2) * g**2
            m_hat    = m_adam / (1 - b1**t_global)   # bias-corrected direction
            v_hat    = v_adam / (1 - b2**t_global)   # bias-corrected scale
            w       -= lr * m_hat / (np.sqrt(v_hat) + eps)   # GPS-guided step

        losses.append(np.mean(epoch_loss))
        y_all = sigmoid(X @ w)
        accs.append(((y_all > 0.5) == y).mean())

    return w, losses, accs

w_final, losses, accs = train_logistic(X_b, y_lr)
```

## Bringing It All Together: The Annotated Training Loop

```python
# Every line commented with the calculus concept it implements
def fully_annotated_training_loop(X, y, lr=0.05, n_epochs=50, batch_size=64):
    """
    A single pass of supervised learning, fully annotated.
    Input X : (n_samples, n_features) — your dataset matrix
    Input y : (n_samples,)            — true labels
    """
    n, d = X.shape
    # Random starting point on the loss surface (the mountain we will descend)
    w = np.zeros(d)

    for epoch in range(n_epochs):
        idx = np.random.permutation(n)   # mini-batch strategy: shuffle each epoch

        for start in range(0, n, batch_size):
            Xb = X[idx[start:start+batch_size]]
            yb = y[idx[start:start+batch_size]]

            # ── FORWARD PASS ──────────────────────────────────────────────────
            # Composite function: ŷ = σ(Xw)
            # = (sigmoid activation) ∘ (linear transform) — chain rule will unwind this
            y_pred = sigmoid(Xb @ w)

            # Loss: how far are we from the bullseye?
            # Binary cross-entropy — derivative designed to be smooth and convex
            loss = -np.mean(yb * np.log(y_pred+1e-9) +
                            (1-yb) * np.log(1-y_pred+1e-9))

            # ── BACKWARD PASS ─────────────────────────────────────────────────
            # Chain rule: ∂L/∂w = (∂L/∂ŷ) × (∂ŷ/∂z) × (∂z/∂w)
            #                    = (error term) × (sigmoid derivative) × (input)
            # This collapses beautifully to: Xᵀ(ŷ - y) / n
            grad = Xb.T @ (y_pred - yb) / len(yb)

            # ── PARAMETER UPDATE ──────────────────────────────────────────────
            # The river flows downhill: step opposite to the gradient
            # Negative gradient = direction of steepest descent on loss surface
            w -= lr * grad   # w ← w − α∇L

    return w
```

## Summary: Calculus as the Engine of Machine Learning

Linear algebra (Article 1) gives machine learning its vocabulary — vectors and matrices to represent data. Calculus gives it its **grammar** — the rules for how learning actually happens.

Here is a plain-English translation of every concept in this article:

| Concept | What it really means | ML Application |
|---------|----------------------|----------------|
| **Derivative** | The speedometer reading of a function at one point | Sensitivity of loss to one weight |
| **Second derivative** | Road curvature — is a valley ahead? | Newton's method, curvature-aware optimisers |
| **Activation derivative** | How much does this neuron pass the error signal along? | Backprop at each layer |
| **Chain rule** | Tracing blame backward through a factory | Backpropagation algorithm |
| **Partial derivative** | Isolating one ingredient's effect | Per-weight gradient in a neural network |
| **Gradient** | The uphill direction on a foggy mountain | Direction and rate of steepest loss increase |
| **Gradient descent** | A river finding the sea | Core training algorithm — step downhill each iteration |
| **Momentum** | A boulder that builds speed downhill | Escape flat regions and local bumps |
| **RMSProp** | Experienced hiker adapting step size to terrain | Adaptive learning rate per parameter |
| **Adam** | GPS combining speed, direction, and road history | Best-of-all-worlds optimiser for deep learning |
| **LR scheduling** | Marathon training plan: warmup → peak → taper | Stable convergence for large models |

The training loop — forward pass, loss, backward pass, update, repeat — is the bicycle-riding child from our introduction, now made precise and automated. Every iteration is one wobble, one fall, one adjustment. Across thousands of iterations, the model learns to ride.
