Every morning a weather app tells you "70% chance of rain". It doesn't know whether it will rain. It is telling you how sure it is. That one sentence is a probability, and it's exactly what most machine learning models give you too: "82% chance this customer will churn", "96% sure this email is spam", "the next word is probably pizza".

Part 1 gave us the shapes (vectors and matrices). Part 2 gave us the engine (derivatives and gradient descent). This part gives us the meaning. It explains what a model's output actually says, why the losses from Part 2 are the "right" ones, how a spam filter can be built from simple counting, and how a language model decides what to say next.

Don't worry if you've found probability confusing before. We'll build everything from coins, dice, deliveries and shopping carts, and every idea comes with a picture you can play with and code you can run.

By the end you will be able to:

  • read and write probabilities, and use the AND, OR and NOT rules without a formula sheet,
  • recognise the handful of distributions ML uses (Bernoulli, Binomial, Poisson, Gaussian) and pick the right one for a business question,
  • work out a conditional probability from a table and use Bayes' theorem without falling into the most common trap,
  • build a Naive Bayes spam filter and a simple fraud detector from scratch,
  • explain maximum likelihood and priors, and why they lead straight to MSE, cross-entropy and regularisation,
  • understand sampling, temperature in language models, entropy, cross-entropy and KL divergence.

Three golden rules for this article

1. A probability is a share. "P = 0.2" means "this happens in about 20 out of every 100 similar situations". When in doubt, turn probabilities into counts of people, orders or days. It makes every problem easier.

2. "Given" changes the crowd. P(A | B) means: throw away everyone who isn't B, then ask how many of the rest are A.

3. Logs turn products into sums. Multiplying many small probabilities gives numbers too tiny for a computer, so ML adds their logs instead. You'll see this again and again.

1What a probability really is

Intuition · the weather forecaster's report card

How would you check whether a forecaster who says "70% chance of rain" is any good? Collect all the days she said 70%. If it rained on about 70 of every 100 of those days, her 70% means something. A probability is a promise about the long run: out of many similar situations, this share will turn out this way.

A few words we'll use:

  • An experiment is anything with an uncertain result: flipping a coin, a customer visiting your site, a delivery leaving the kitchen.
  • An outcome is one possible result: heads; "bought"; "arrived in 32 minutes".
  • An event is a group of outcomes you care about: "rolled an even number"; "arrived late".

The share of heads in a few flips can be far from 50%, but it settles down as the flips pile up. This is called the law of large numbers, and it's why companies trust probabilities estimated from lots of data.

Flip a coin, many times

Set how biased the coin is, then flip. The line shows the share of heads so far. Early on it jumps around; with more flips it settles near the true value.

Python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
flips = rng.integers(0, 2, size=10_000)       # 1 = heads, 0 = tails

for n in [10, 100, 1_000, 10_000]:
    print(f"after {n:>6,} flips: share of heads = {flips[:n].mean():.3f}")
Output
after     10 flips: share of heads = 0.400
after    100 flips: share of heads = 0.520
after  1,000 flips: share of heads = 0.509
after 10,000 flips: share of heads = 0.494

The three rules you'll use all the time

Math, decoded
NOT: P(not A) = 1 − P(A) OR: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B) AND: P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B) ← only if A and B don't affect each other (independent)

Why subtract in the OR rule? Because outcomes that are both A and B get counted twice when you add P(A) and P(B). Take one copy away.

By hand · one die
Outcomes: 1 2 3 4 5 6 (each 1/6) "even" = {2, 4, 6} P = 3/6 = 0.5 "big" = {5, 6} P = 2/6 ≈ 0.333 "even AND big" = {6} P = 1/6 ≈ 0.167 "even OR big" = {2,4,5,6} P = 0.5 + 0.333 − 0.167 = 0.667 (6 was counted twice) "not a 6" P = 1 − 1/6 ≈ 0.833
Python
die = np.array([1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6])     # every equally likely outcome
even = die % 2 == 0                     # the event "even": 2, 4, 6
big  = die > 4                          # the event "big": 5, 6

print("P(even)         =", round(even.mean(), 3))
print("P(not a 6)      =", round(1 - (die == 6).mean(), 3))
print("P(even AND big) =", round((even & big).mean(), 3))
print("P(even OR big)  =", round((even | big).mean(), 3),
      "  check:", round(even.mean() + big.mean() - (even & big).mean(), 3))
Output
P(even)         = 0.5
P(not a 6)      = 0.833
P(even AND big) = 0.167
P(even OR big)  = 0.667   check: 0.667
In ML

A classifier's output "0.82" is a probability, so it should behave like the forecaster: among all customers given 0.82, about 82% should actually churn. When that holds, the model is called well calibrated. Many models aren't, out of the box, which is why tools like calibration curves exist.

In business

Probabilities let you plan with uncertainty instead of ignoring it. "There's a 30% chance this shipment is late" is far more useful to an operations team than a flat "it might be late", because they can compare it with the cost of a backup plan.

Check yourself

1. P(customer opens the email) = 0.25. What is P(doesn't open it)? Answer: 0.75.

2. You flip a fair coin twice. What's P(two heads)? Answer: 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25. The flips are independent.

3. After 10 flips you got 8 heads. Is the coin unfair? Answer: not necessarily. With only 10 flips, the share jumps around a lot; you'd need many more flips to be confident.

2Random variables and distributions

A random variable is just a number that comes out of an uncertain process: how many visitors click an ad today, how many orders arrive in the next hour. Its distribution is the full list of possible values and how likely each one is.

Intuition · a distribution is a recipe for surprise

A shop owner knows roughly how many customers to expect on a Tuesday. Usually 40 to 60, rarely under 30, almost never 100. That mental picture, "which numbers are common and which are rare", is a distribution. Writing it down precisely lets a computer plan with it.

You don't need dozens of distributions. Four cover most of everyday ML:

DistributionDescribesExample questionIts setting
BernoulliOne yes/no eventWill this visitor click?p = chance of yes
BinomialHow many yeses out of n triesHow many of 100 visitors click?n tries, chance p each
CategoricalOne choice among several optionsWill they pay by UPI, card or cash?one probability per option
PoissonCounts of events in a time windowHow many orders in the next hour?λ (lambda) = average count

Distribution explorer

Pick a distribution and move its settings. Each bar is the probability of that exact value.

Binomial: counting successes

Each visitor clicks with probability 5%, independently. Out of 100 visitors, how many clicks should you expect on a given day? About 5, but some days 2 and some days 9. The Binomial formula gives the chance of each exact count:

Math, decoded
P(exactly k successes) = C(n, k) × pᵏ × (1 − p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ

pᵏ: k visitors click. (1 − p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ: the other n − k don't. C(n, k) ("n choose k"): the number of different ways to pick which k visitors clicked.

Python
from math import comb

p_click = 0.05                          # each visitor clicks the ad with probability 5%
n = 100                                 # 100 visitors today

for k in [0, 3, 5, 8, 10]:
    prob = comb(n, k) * p_click**k * (1 - p_click)**(n - k)    # Binomial formula
    print(f"P(exactly {k:2d} clicks) = {prob:.3f}")

days = rng.binomial(n, p_click, size=100_000)                  # simulate 100,000 days
print("simulated share of days with exactly 5 clicks:", round(np.mean(days == 5), 3))
Output
P(exactly  0 clicks) = 0.006
P(exactly  3 clicks) = 0.140
P(exactly  5 clicks) = 0.180
P(exactly  8 clicks) = 0.065
P(exactly 10 clicks) = 0.017
simulated share of days with exactly 5 clicks: 0.18

Poisson: counting arrivals

When events arrive one by one with no fixed number of "tries" (orders, support tickets, website errors), independently of each other and at a steady average rate, the Poisson distribution fits. It needs only one number: the average count λ. (If the rate changes through the day, as lunch-time rushes do, use a separate λ for each hour.)

Python
from math import exp, factorial

avg_orders = 4                          # a small cafe gets 4 orders per hour on average

for k in range(0, 10, 2):
    prob = avg_orders**k * exp(-avg_orders) / factorial(k)     # Poisson formula
    print(f"P({k} orders in an hour) = {prob:.3f}")

p_8_or_fewer = sum(avg_orders**k * exp(-avg_orders) / factorial(k) for k in range(9))
print(f"P(more than 8 orders)  = {1 - p_8_or_fewer:.3f}")
Output
P(0 orders in an hour) = 0.018
P(2 orders in an hour) = 0.147
P(4 orders in an hour) = 0.195
P(6 orders in an hour) = 0.104
P(8 orders in an hour) = 0.030
P(more than 8 orders)  = 0.021

Even with an average of 4 orders an hour, there's a 2% chance of a rush of 9 or more. If the cafe staffs only for the average, it will be swamped a few times a month.

In ML

Choosing a distribution is choosing a model's output type. A yes/no classifier outputs a Bernoulli probability (Part 2's sigmoid). A multi-class classifier outputs a Categorical distribution (Part 2's softmax). A model predicting counts, such as daily demand, often uses a Poisson output; scikit-learn's PoissonRegressor and XGBoost's count:poisson objective exist for exactly this.

In business

Staffing call centres, stocking shelves and sizing servers are Poisson problems. Plan for the average and you'll be short-staffed regularly. Plan for, say, the 95th percentile of the distribution and you'll cover all but the rarest rushes. The distribution turns a gut feeling into a number you can budget for.

Check yourself

1. Which distribution fits "how many of 20 loan applicants will default"? Answer: Binomial (n = 20 tries, each with a default probability).

2. Which fits "how many website errors in the next 10 minutes"? Answer: Poisson.

3Continuous values and the bell curve

Some quantities aren't counts: delivery times, heights, prices. They can take any value, like 31.274 minutes. For these we use a smooth curve instead of bars.

Intuition · probability is area, not height

Imagine sand poured along a ruler, piled high where values are common and thin where they're rare. The chance that a delivery takes between 25 and 35 minutes is the amount of sand between those two marks. The height of the pile at one exact point isn't a probability; it only tells you how crowded that area is. That height is called the probability density.

The normal (Gaussian) distribution

The most famous curve is the normal distribution, the bell curve. It appears, at least approximately, whenever many small, independent effects add up (traffic lights, kitchen speed and road conditions all nudging a delivery time). This is the central limit theorem. Real delivery times can't go below zero and often have a longer right tail, so the bell curve is a convenient approximation, not a law. It has two settings: the mean μ (where the peak is) and the standard deviation σ (how wide the bell is).

The 68–95–99.7 rule

For any normal distribution:

  • about 68% of values fall within 1 σ of the mean,
  • about 95% within 2 σ,
  • about 99.7% within 3 σ.

Deliveries averaging 30 minutes with σ = 5: about 68% arrive in 25–35 minutes, 95% in 20–40, and almost all in 15–45.

The bell curve

Set the average and the spread, then choose a range. The shaded area is the probability of landing in that range.

Python
from math import erf, sqrt

def normal_prob(a, b, mean, sd):
    """Chance that a normal value lands between a and b."""
    cdf = lambda x: 0.5 * (1 + erf((x - mean) / (sd * sqrt(2))))
    return cdf(b) - cdf(a)

mean, sd = 30, 5                        # delivery time in minutes
for lo, hi in [(25, 35), (20, 40), (15, 45)]:
    print(f"P({lo} to {hi} min) = {normal_prob(lo, hi, mean, sd):.3f}")
print(f"P(over 40 min)   = {normal_prob(40, 1e9, mean, sd):.3f}")

times = rng.normal(mean, sd, 100_000)  # check by simulating 100,000 deliveries
print(f"simulated P(over 40 min) = {np.mean(times > 40):.3f}")
Output
P(25 to 35 min) = 0.683
P(20 to 40 min) = 0.954
P(15 to 45 min) = 0.997
P(over 40 min)   = 0.023
simulated P(over 40 min) = 0.023

The formula and the 100,000 simulated deliveries agree: only 2.3% take longer than 40 minutes. A promise of "40 minutes or it's free" would cost the business on about 1 order in 44.

Common mistake · density is not probability

The height of the curve can be bigger than 1. Probabilities can't. Squeeze the bell narrow and its peak must rise so that the total area stays exactly 1:

Python
from math import pi

def normal_density(x, mean, sd):
    return np.exp(-0.5 * ((x - mean) / sd) ** 2) / (sd * np.sqrt(2 * pi))

print("density at the peak, sd = 5  :", round(normal_density(30, 30, 5), 3))
print("density at the peak, sd = 0.1:", round(normal_density(30, 30, 0.1), 3), " ← bigger than 1, and that's fine")
Output
density at the peak, sd = 5  : 0.08
density at the peak, sd = 0.1: 3.989  ← bigger than 1, and that's fine

A link back to Part 1: standardising a feature (subtract the mean, divide by σ) turns any normal variable into the standard normal, with mean 0 and σ = 1. A z-score of 2 means "two standard deviations above average", higher than about 97.7% of all values.

In ML

The Gaussian is everywhere: weights in a neural network start as small random numbers, often drawn from a Gaussian (Part 2's He and Xavier initialisation come in Gaussian and uniform versions), linear regression assumes Gaussian noise (Section 9 shows why that gives MSE), anomaly detectors flag points far out in the tails, and VAEs and diffusion models generate images by transforming Gaussian noise.

In business

Service-level promises are tail questions: "what share of deliveries will break our 40-minute promise?", "how much stock covers 95% of weeks?". Once you know the mean and spread, the bell curve answers them in one line.

Check yourself

1. Heights average 165 cm with σ = 7. Roughly what share is between 151 and 179 cm? Answer: about 95% (within 2 σ).

2. What is the probability that a delivery takes exactly 30.000 minutes? Answer: zero. For continuous values only ranges have probability.

4Expected value and variance

Intuition · the scratch-card promotion

A store plans a scratch card for every customer: 90% win nothing, 9% win ₹100, 1% win ₹500. What does each card cost the store? Not ₹0 (most cards), not ₹500 (the best one), but the long-run average over many cards. That average is the expected value.

Math, decoded
E[X] = Σ value × probability the long-run average Var(X) = E[(X − E[X])²] average squared distance from it σ = √Var(X) spread, in the original units

"Multiply each value by its probability and add" is a dot product of the values and the probabilities, exactly as in Part 1.

By hand
E = 0 × 0.90 + 100 × 0.09 + 500 × 0.01 = 0 + 9 + 5 = ₹14 per card
Python
prizes = np.array([0, 100, 500])       # ₹ value printed on a scratch card
chance = np.array([0.90, 0.09, 0.01])  # how often each one appears

expected = prizes @ chance              # Σ value × probability: a dot product (Part 1)
variance = ((prizes - expected) ** 2) @ chance
print(f"expected cost per card: ₹{expected:.2f}")
print(f"standard deviation    : ₹{np.sqrt(variance):.2f}")

cards = rng.choice(prizes, size=100_000, p=chance)              # hand out 100,000 cards
print(f"average over 100,000 simulated cards: ₹{cards.mean():.2f}")
Output
expected cost per card: ₹14.00
standard deviation    : ₹56.60
average over 100,000 simulated cards: ₹13.74

₹14 per card on average, but with a standard deviation of ₹56.60: individual cards vary wildly, from ₹0 to ₹500. The simulation lands close to ₹14, and it would land closer with more cards. Expected value tells the finance team what to budget. Variance tells them how bumpy the ride will be.

Why averages are steadier than single values

One delivery can be 20 minutes or 40. The average of 256 deliveries hardly moves. For independent values, the spread of an average shrinks like σ ÷ √n: four times the data halves the wobble. (This number, σ ÷ √n, is called the standard error.)

Python
delivery_times = rng.normal(30, 5, size=1_000_000)    # a big "population" of deliveries

for batch in [1, 16, 256]:
    means = [rng.choice(delivery_times, batch).mean() for _ in range(2_000)]
    print(f"batch of {batch:3d}: averages spread by ±{np.std(means):.2f} min   (5 / √{batch} = {5 / np.sqrt(batch):.2f})")
Output
batch of   1: averages spread by ±5.06 min   (5 / √1 = 5.00)
batch of  16: averages spread by ±1.23 min   (5 / √16 = 1.25)
batch of 256: averages spread by ±0.31 min   (5 / √256 = 0.31)
In ML

This is the probability behind Part 2's mini-batches. The true loss is the expected loss over all possible data; a mini-batch gradient is an average over a sample, so it's a noisy estimate whose noise shrinks like 1 ÷ √(batch size). Batch 256 isn't 16× better than batch 16; it's √16 = 4× less noisy, for 16× the compute. That trade-off (together with how much a GPU can process at once) is a big part of why batch sizes of 32 to 512 are so common.

In business

Expected value is the backbone of decisions under uncertainty: expected profit of a promotion, expected loss on a loan (probability of default × amount lost), expected revenue of a sales pipeline (deal size × chance to close). Variance tells you how much buffer to keep for bad months.

Check yourself

1. A loan of ₹2 lakh has a 4% chance of default, losing everything if it does. What's the expected loss? Answer: 0.04 × 2,00,000 = ₹8,000.

2. Going from a batch of 32 to 128 reduces the noise in the average by what factor? Answer: √4 = 2.

5Joint, marginal and conditional probability

Real questions involve two things at once: device and purchase, plan and churn, age and default. A simple table of counts answers almost all of them.

Intuition · "given" means "shrink the crowd"

1,000 people visited your shop. To find "the chance someone buys, given they're on mobile", don't look at all 1,000 people. Walk over to the mobile visitors only, and ask what share of them bought. Conditional probability is just a share inside a smaller group.

1,000 visitsBoughtDidn't buyTotal

Math, decoded
TypeQuestionFrom the table
JointP(mobile and buy)120 / 1,000 = 0.12
MarginalP(buy), ignoring device220 / 1,000 = 0.22 (the row/column totals, the "margins")
ConditionalP(buy | mobile)120 / 600 = 0.20 (only the mobile row)
P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B) "the joint share, inside group B"
Python
#                bought  didn't buy
counts = np.array([[120,     480],     # mobile visitors
                   [100,     300]])    # desktop visitors
total = counts.sum()

p_mobile          = counts[0].sum() / total
p_buy             = counts[:, 0].sum() / total
p_mobile_and_buy  = counts[0, 0] / total
p_buy_if_mobile   = counts[0, 0] / counts[0].sum()          # look only at the mobile row
p_buy_if_desktop  = counts[1, 0] / counts[1].sum()

print(f"P(mobile) = {p_mobile:.2f}   P(buy) = {p_buy:.2f}   P(mobile and buy) = {p_mobile_and_buy:.2f}")
print(f"P(buy | mobile) = {p_buy_if_mobile:.2f}   P(buy | desktop) = {p_buy_if_desktop:.2f}")
print(f"independent?  P(mobile)·P(buy) = {p_mobile * p_buy:.3f}  vs  P(mobile and buy) = {p_mobile_and_buy:.3f}")
Output
P(mobile) = 0.60   P(buy) = 0.22   P(mobile and buy) = 0.12
P(buy | mobile) = 0.20   P(buy | desktop) = 0.25
independent?  P(mobile)·P(buy) = 0.132  vs  P(mobile and buy) = 0.120

Mobile visitors buy 20% of the time, desktop visitors 25%. So device and buying are not independent: knowing the device changes the odds. The quick test agrees: if they were independent, P(mobile) × P(buy) would equal P(mobile and buy), but 0.132 ≠ 0.120.

Common mistake · P(A | B) is not P(B | A)

P(buy | mobile) = 120/600 = 0.20, but P(mobile | buy) = 120/220 ≈ 0.55. "Most buyers use mobile" and "most mobile users buy" are completely different claims. Mixing them up is one of the most common errors in business reporting, and Section 6 shows how costly it can be.

In ML

A classifier's output is a conditional probability: P(churn | this customer's features). Naive Bayes (Section 7) is built entirely from conditional probabilities. And the "independence" idea matters a lot: many algorithms assume features or examples are independent, and results go wrong quietly when they aren't.

In business

Every "conversion rate by channel" or "default rate by region" in a dashboard is a conditional probability. The table view also exposes a trap: a channel with a high conversion rate but tiny traffic may matter less than a mediocre channel with huge traffic. Look at the joint counts, not just the conditional rates.

Check yourself

1. From the table, what is P(desktop | didn't buy)? Answer: 300 / 780 ≈ 0.38.

2. If P(A | B) = P(A), what does that tell you? Answer: A and B are independent; knowing B doesn't change the chance of A.

6Bayes' theorem: updating beliefs with evidence

Story · the fraud alert that's usually wrong

A bank's fraud system catches 95% of frauds and wrongly flags only 3% of honest payments. It sounds excellent. Your card payment gets flagged. What's the chance it's really fraud? Most people guess "about 95%". The true answer is about 14%. The reason is that fraud is rare: only 0.5% of payments are fraudulent, so the few frauds are buried under false alarms from the huge number of honest payments.

Math, decoded
P(fraud | alert) = P(alert | fraud) × P(fraud) / P(alert) prior P(fraud) what you believed before the evidence (the base rate) likelihood P(alert | fraud) how well the evidence fits "fraud" posterior P(fraud | alert) what you should believe after the evidence

Bayes' theorem is the conditional-probability formula from Section 5, flipped around. It lets you turn "how likely is the evidence if it's fraud" into "how likely is fraud given the evidence".

By hand · count 100,000 payments
fraud: 0.5% of 100,000 = 500 → 95% flagged = 475 alerts honest: 99.5% = 99,500 → 3% flagged = 2,985 alerts ───── total alerts 3,460 P(fraud | alert) = 475 / 3,460 ≈ 0.137

Counting people (or payments) instead of juggling percentages is the easiest way to do any Bayes problem.

Python
p_fraud       = 0.005    # 0.5% of transactions are fraud (the base rate)
p_alert_fraud = 0.95     # the system flags 95% of frauds
p_alert_legit = 0.03     # ...but also flags 3% of honest transactions

p_alert = p_alert_fraud * p_fraud + p_alert_legit * (1 - p_fraud)
p_fraud_given_alert = p_alert_fraud * p_fraud / p_alert          # Bayes' theorem
print(f"P(fraud | alert) = {p_fraud_given_alert:.3f}")

# Same answer by simply counting 100,000 transactions
fraud = rng.random(100_000) < p_fraud
alert = np.where(fraud, rng.random(100_000) < p_alert_fraud, rng.random(100_000) < p_alert_legit)
print(f"alerts: {alert.sum():,}   of which real fraud: {(alert & fraud).sum():,}   share = {(alert & fraud).sum() / alert.sum():.3f}")
Output
P(fraud | alert) = 0.137
alerts: 3,457   of which real fraud: 474   share = 0.137

Things to try: raise the base rate to 10% and the same alarm becomes much more trustworthy. Lower false alarms to 0.5% and precision jumps. For rare events, cutting false alarms matters more than catching a few more true cases.

In ML

This is the probability behind precision and class imbalance. When the positive class is rare (fraud, disease, defects), even a model with high accuracy can produce mostly false alarms, so accuracy is a misleading metric and precision, recall and PR curves matter. Bayes' theorem also powers Naive Bayes, Bayesian A/B testing, and any model that combines prior knowledge with data.

In business

Every alerting system faces this: fraud flags, machine-failure warnings, churn alerts sent to account managers. If 86% of alerts are false, teams stop trusting them. Bayes tells you the precision you'll get before you launch, from the base rate and the two error rates.

Check yourself

1. A test catches 90% of a rare defect (1% of items) and false-alarms on 10% of good items. Of 1,000 items, how many alerts, and how many real? Answer: 9 real + 99 false = 108 alerts; only 9/108 ≈ 8% are real.

2. In Bayes' theorem, what is the "prior"? Answer: what you believed before seeing the evidence, here the base rate of fraud.

7Naive Bayes: a spam filter from counting words

Bayes' theorem becomes a real classifier with one bold simplification. To decide whether a message is spam, look at its words and ask: are these words more typical of spam or of normal ("ham") messages?

Intuition · a detective weighing clues

A detective doesn't decide on one clue. "Free" is a mild hint of spam. "Cash" is another. "Win" is another. Each clue nudges the odds, and together they add up to a verdict. Naive Bayes treats every word as a separate clue and simply combines their nudges.

Math, decoded
P(spam | words) ∝ P(spam) × P(word₁ | spam) × P(word₂ | spam) × …
  • ∝ means "is proportional to": we compute the same thing for "ham" and compare the two.
  • "Naive": it assumes each word appears independently of the others, given the class. That's obviously not true ("free" and "prize" often travel together), but the classifier works surprisingly well anyway.
  • Laplace smoothing: add 1 to every word count, so a word never seen in spam doesn't make P(spam) exactly zero.
  • Logs: multiplying many small probabilities underflows, so we add log-probabilities (golden rule 3).
Python
from collections import Counter

spam = ["win cash now", "free prize win", "claim free cash", "win a free phone"]
ham  = ["meeting at noon", "lunch now", "project update at noon", "call me about the project"]

def word_counts(msgs):
    return Counter(w for m in msgs for w in m.split())

spam_counts, ham_counts = word_counts(spam), word_counts(ham)
vocab = set(spam_counts) | set(ham_counts)

def log_score(message, counts, prior):
    total = sum(counts.values())
    score = np.log(prior)
    for w in message.split():
        p_word = (counts[w] + 1) / (total + len(vocab))     # +1 = Laplace smoothing
        score += np.log(p_word)                             # add logs instead of multiplying
    return score

for msg in ["free cash now", "project meeting now"]:
    s, h = log_score(msg, spam_counts, 0.5), log_score(msg, ham_counts, 0.5)
    p_spam = 1 / (1 + np.exp(h - s))                        # turn the two scores into a probability
    print(f"{msg!r:24s} → P(spam) = {p_spam:.3f}")
Output
'free cash now'          → P(spam) = 0.930
'project meeting now'    → P(spam) = 0.155

"free cash now" scores 93% spam; "project meeting now" only 15.5%. Eight training messages and a few lines of counting. To make sure our hand-made version is right, compare it with scikit-learn's implementation:

Python
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import CountVectorizer
from sklearn.naive_bayes import MultinomialNB

vec = CountVectorizer(token_pattern=r"\b\w+\b")
X_train = vec.fit_transform(spam + ham)
nb = MultinomialNB(alpha=1.0).fit(X_train, [1] * 4 + [0] * 4)

print("scikit-learn P(spam):", nb.predict_proba(vec.transform(["free cash now", "project meeting now"]))[:, 1].round(3))
Output
scikit-learn P(spam): [0.93  0.155]

Identical probabilities. You now know exactly what MultinomialNB computes.

In ML

Naive Bayes is fast, needs little data, and is a strong baseline for text classification (spam, sentiment, topic tagging). Its independence assumption also makes it a useful teaching contrast: logistic regression (Part 2) learns how much each word matters directly, without assuming independence, and usually wins once you have more data.

In business

Email spam filters, routing support tickets by keywords, flagging product reviews that mention "refund" or "broken": Naive Bayes trains in seconds on a laptop, and its word probabilities are easy to show to a non-technical stakeholder ("messages with 'refund' are 6× more likely to be complaints").

Check yourself

1. Why do we add 1 to every word count? Answer: so an unseen word doesn't give a probability of exactly zero, which would wipe out every other clue.

2. What does the "naive" in Naive Bayes assume? Answer: that the words are independent of each other once you know the class.

8Two features at once: the multivariate Gaussian

Real data has several features that move together. Bigger orders usually contain more items. Taller people usually weigh more. The multivariate Gaussian describes a cloud of points like this, using a mean vector and the covariance matrix from Part 1.

Intuition · "unusual" depends on the pattern

A ₹4,000 order with 8 items is big, but it fits the pattern: lots of items, lots of money. A ₹4,000 order with just 1 item breaks the pattern, even though neither number is extreme on its own. Spotting fraud means judging the combination, not each feature separately.

Math, decoded
x ~ N(μ, Σ) μ = mean vector, Σ = covariance matrix (Part 1, Section 12) Mahalanobis distance: d(x) = √( (x − μ)ᵀ Σ⁻¹ (x − μ) )

The bell curve becomes a hill whose contour lines are ellipses tilted along the correlation. The Mahalanobis distance measures how far a point is from the centre in units of the cloud's own shape: a step along the stretched direction counts little; a step across it counts a lot.

How unusual is this order?

Change the correlation, then drag the coral point. Plain distance draws circles; Mahalanobis distance follows the ellipses.

Python
mean = [2.0, 4.0]                        # order value (₹ thousand), number of items
cov  = [[1.0, 1.6],
        [1.6, 4.0]]                      # big orders tend to have more items (correlation 0.8)
orders = rng.multivariate_normal(mean, cov, size=5_000)

mu = orders.mean(axis=0)
C  = np.cov(orders, rowvar=False)        # the covariance matrix (Part 1, Section 12)
C_inv = np.linalg.inv(C)
sd = orders.std(axis=0)

def plain_distance(x):  return np.sqrt(np.sum(((x - mu) / sd) ** 2))        # ignores correlation
def mahalanobis(x):     d = x - mu; return np.sqrt(d @ C_inv @ d)           # respects it

for name, x in [("typical order",          [2.2, 4.5]),
                ("big but consistent",     [4.0, 8.0]),
                ("big value, only 1 item", [4.0, 1.0])]:
    x = np.array(x)
    print(f"{name:24s} plain = {plain_distance(x):.2f}   Mahalanobis = {mahalanobis(x):.2f}")
Output
typical order            plain = 0.32   Mahalanobis = 0.25
big but consistent       plain = 2.84   Mahalanobis = 2.12
big value, only 1 item   plain = 2.52   Mahalanobis = 5.59

Plain distance ranks "big but consistent" (2.84) as stranger than "big value, only 1 item" (2.52). Mahalanobis distance gets it right: the 1-item order is more than twice as unusual (5.59 vs 2.12), because it goes against the correlation.

In ML

Gaussian anomaly detection, Gaussian Discriminant Analysis, Gaussian Mixture Models (a "soft" version of k-means), Gaussian processes and the latent space of VAEs all build on the multivariate Gaussian. It's also the reason correlated features matter so much: the covariance matrix holds the whole story of how features move together.

In business

Fraud and anomaly teams use exactly this idea: an expense claim, a transaction or a sensor reading is suspicious not because one number is large, but because the combination is unlike anything normal. Mahalanobis-style scores catch "normal-looking numbers in an abnormal mix".

Check yourself

1. Two features are perfectly uncorrelated and standardised. How do Mahalanobis and plain (standardised) distance compare? Answer: they are the same; the covariance matrix is the identity.

2. What shape are the contour lines of a 2-D Gaussian with correlated features? Answer: tilted ellipses.

9Maximum likelihood: which settings best explain the data?

So far we knew the probabilities and asked about the data. Machine learning works the other way round: we have the data and want the settings (the model's weights). Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) is the most common way to do that, and it is where Part 2's loss functions come from.

Intuition · the detective's best explanation

A friend flips a coin 10 times and gets 7 heads. Which coin is it most likely to be: a fair one (50% heads), a 70% coin or a 90% coin? Ask each candidate: "if you were the coin, how likely would 7 heads out of 10 be?" The candidate that makes the observed data most likely wins. That's maximum likelihood.

Math, decoded
likelihood L(p) = P(data | p) = p⁷ × (1 − p)³ for 7 heads, 3 tails log-likelihood log L(p) = 7·log(p) + 3·log(1 − p) MLE p̂ = the p that makes L(p) as big as possible = 7/10

The hat on p̂ means "estimated from data". Setting the derivative of the log-likelihood to zero (Part 2, Section 5) gives p̂ = heads ÷ flips: the obvious answer, now with a reason behind it.

Common mistake · likelihood is not "the probability that p is true"

L(0.7) answers "if the coin were a 70% coin, how likely is this data?". It does not say "there is an X% chance the coin is a 70% coin". The likelihood curve doesn't even add up to 1 over p. Turning it into a genuine probability for p needs a prior and Bayes' theorem, which is what Section 10 does.

Find the most likely coin

Choose how many heads you saw. The curve shows how well each possible coin explains it; its peak is the MLE. The prior slider is for Section 10.

Python
heads, flips = 7, 10
p_grid = np.linspace(0.01, 0.99, 99)                   # candidate values for the coin's bias

likelihood = p_grid**heads * (1 - p_grid)**(flips - heads)
log_lik    = heads * np.log(p_grid) + (flips - heads) * np.log(1 - p_grid)

print("p with the highest likelihood    :", p_grid[np.argmax(likelihood)].round(2))
print("p with the highest log-likelihood:", p_grid[np.argmax(log_lik)].round(2))
print("simple share of heads            :", heads / flips)
Output
p with the highest likelihood    : 0.7
p with the highest log-likelihood: 0.7
simple share of heads            : 0.7

Why everyone uses the log-likelihood

With lots of data, the likelihood is a product of many probabilities, each below 1. The product quickly becomes too small for a computer to store. Logs turn the product into a sum and keep everything well-behaved. The peak stays in the same place, because log never changes which value is biggest.

Python
probs = np.full(1000, 0.3)                 # 1,000 data points, each given probability 0.3
print("product of probabilities:", np.prod(probs))          # too small for a computer: 0.0
print("sum of log-probabilities:", round(np.sum(np.log(probs)), 2))
Output
product of probabilities: 0.0
sum of log-probabilities: -1203.97

The big payoff: Part 2's losses are maximum likelihood

Here is something many courses never say clearly. The loss functions you minimised in Part 2 weren't arbitrary choices. Each one is negative log-likelihood under a simple assumption about the data:

If you assume……maximising likelihood is the same as minimisingUsed for
Targets = prediction + Gaussian noise (of a fixed size)Mean squared errorRegression
Targets are yes/no (Bernoulli)Binary cross-entropyLogistic regression
Targets are one of k classes (Categorical)Softmax cross-entropyClassifiers, language models
Targets are counts (Poisson)Poisson lossDemand forecasting

First check, using Part 2's ad-spend data: the weight with the lowest MSE and the weight with the highest Gaussian likelihood are the same.

Python
rng7 = np.random.default_rng(7)                          # the ad-spend data from Part 2
ad_spend = rng7.uniform(1, 10, 20).round(1)
sales = (3.0 * ad_spend + 8 + rng7.normal(0, 2, 20)).round(1)

w_grid = np.linspace(2.5, 4.0, 1501)
mse = [np.mean((w * ad_spend + 5.5 - sales) ** 2) for w in w_grid]

sigma = 2.0                                               # assumed noise level
log_lik = [np.sum(-0.5 * ((sales - (w * ad_spend + 5.5)) / sigma) ** 2
                  - np.log(sigma * np.sqrt(2 * np.pi))) for w in w_grid]

print("w with the lowest MSE           :", w_grid[np.argmin(mse)].round(3))
print("w with the highest likelihood   :", w_grid[np.argmax(log_lik)].round(3))
Output
w with the lowest MSE           : 3.248
w with the highest likelihood   : 3.248

Second check: for yes/no outcomes, the negative log-likelihood and Part 2's binary cross-entropy are literally the same number.

Python
y = np.array([1, 0, 1, 1, 0])               # what happened (1 = defaulted)
p = np.array([0.9, 0.2, 0.7, 0.6, 0.1])     # the model's predicted probability of 1

# Negative log-likelihood: −log(chance the model gave to what actually happened)
nll = -np.sum(np.log(np.where(y == 1, p, 1 - p)))
bce = -np.sum(y * np.log(p) + (1 - y) * np.log(1 - p))   # the cross-entropy formula from Part 2
print(f"negative log-likelihood = {nll:.4f}")
print(f"binary cross-entropy    = {bce:.4f}")
Output
negative log-likelihood = 1.3014
binary cross-entropy    = 1.3014
In ML

Training almost any model is maximum likelihood in disguise: logistic regression, neural network classifiers, and large language models, which are trained to maximise the likelihood of the next token in billions of sentences. When you need a loss for a new kind of target, ask "what distribution describes this target?" and take its negative log-likelihood.

In business

Picking the loss is picking an assumption about your data. Demand counts of 0, 1, 2, 3 per store-day aren't Gaussian, and a Poisson loss usually forecasts them better than MSE. Knowing the link between distributions and losses lets you choose on purpose instead of by default.

Check yourself

1. 45 of 200 customers clicked. What is the MLE of the click rate? Answer: 45/200 = 0.225.

2. Which loss does maximum likelihood give for yes/no targets? Answer: binary cross-entropy.

10Priors and MAP: common sense for small data

Story · the three-flip coin

You flip a new coin 3 times and get 3 heads. Maximum likelihood says the coin lands heads 100% of the time. No sensible person believes that: you know most coins are roughly fair, and 3 flips is very little evidence. A prior is a way of writing down that common sense, so the model doesn't over-react to small samples.

Math, decoded
MLE: maximise P(data | θ) MAP: maximise P(data | θ) × P(θ) ← P(θ) is the prior

MAP ("maximum a posteriori") picks the settings that are most believable after combining the data with the prior, which is Bayes' theorem from Section 6 applied to model weights. For a coin, a simple prior acts like pretend flips: start as if you'd already seen 2 heads and 2 tails. (In technical terms this is the MAP estimate under a Beta(3, 3) prior; the pretend-flips picture is all you need to use it.)

Python
def estimate(heads, flips, prior_heads=0, prior_tails=0):
    """MLE when the prior counts are 0; MAP with 'pretend' prior counts otherwise."""
    return (heads + prior_heads) / (flips + prior_heads + prior_tails)

print("3 flips, 3 heads    MLE:", estimate(3, 3),
      "   MAP:", round(estimate(3, 3, prior_heads=2, prior_tails=2), 3))
print("300 flips, 210 heads MLE:", estimate(210, 300),
      "   MAP:", round(estimate(210, 300, prior_heads=2, prior_tails=2), 3))
Output
3 flips, 3 heads    MLE: 1.0    MAP: 0.714
300 flips, 210 heads MLE: 0.7    MAP: 0.697

With 3 flips, the prior pulls the estimate from an absurd 100% down to a reasonable 71%. With 300 flips, it barely matters (0.697 vs 0.700): the data has taken over. That's exactly how a prior should behave. Try it with the "prior" slider in the Section 9 widget: set it to 2 and drag the flips down to 3.

Priors are regularisation

This is the second big link to Part 2. Take the log of the MAP formula and you get "log-likelihood + log-prior". The log-prior becomes a penalty on the weights:

Prior belief about each weightBecomes the penaltyKnown as
Gaussian around 0: "weights are probably small"λ·Σ wᵢ²L2 / Ridge / weight decay
Laplace, sharply peaked at 0: "most weights are probably zero or tiny"λ·Σ |wᵢ|L1 / Lasso (its MAP answer sets many weights to exactly 0)
Add-one pseudo-counts on word frequenciesLaplace smoothingNaive Bayes (Section 7)

The MAP solution with a Gaussian prior and scikit-learn's Ridge regression give the same weights. The penalty strength is λ = σ² ÷ τ²: the noise variance divided by the prior's variance, so a tighter prior (small τ) means stronger regularisation.

Python
from sklearn.linear_model import Ridge

rng3 = np.random.default_rng(3)
X = rng3.normal(size=(30, 4))
y = X @ np.array([2.0, -1.0, 0.0, 0.5]) + rng3.normal(0, 1, 30)

lam = 5.0
w_map = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X + lam * np.eye(4), X.T @ y)   # MAP with a Gaussian prior on w
w_ridge = Ridge(alpha=lam, fit_intercept=False).fit(X, y).coef_
print("MAP (Gaussian prior):", w_map.round(4))
print("Ridge regression    :", w_ridge.round(4))
Output
MAP (Gaussian prior): [ 1.7988 -1.0451  0.0753  0.3181]
Ridge regression    : [ 1.7988 -1.0451  0.0753  0.3181]
In ML

Every time you tune alpha in Ridge or Lasso, or weight_decay in PyTorch, you're choosing how strong your prior is. A stronger prior helps when data is small or noisy; with huge datasets the data dominates and the prior matters less. The full Bayesian approach goes further and keeps a whole distribution over weights instead of one best value, which gives uncertainty estimates.

In business

A new product with 2 sales and 2 five-star reviews shouldn't outrank a product with 900 reviews averaging 4.7. Ranking by a "smoothed" rating (adding a few pretend average reviews, often called a Bayesian average) is MAP-style thinking in action, and it's a common way to stop tiny samples from topping a chart.

Check yourself

1. A product has 1 review: 5 stars. With 4 pretend reviews of 3 stars added, what's its smoothed average? Answer: (5 + 12) / 5 = 3.4.

2. Which prior corresponds to L2 regularisation? Answer: a Gaussian prior centred at zero.

11Sampling and simulation

Sometimes a probability is too messy to work out with a formula. Then you can let the computer play out the situation thousands of times and count. This is called Monte Carlo simulation, named after the famous casino in Monaco.

Intuition · a rehearsal, many times over

Before opening night, a theatre rehearses. A simulation rehearses your business 100,000 times in a second: random kitchen time, random road time, random traffic. Count how many rehearsals ran late, and you have the probability of being late.

Python
n = 100_000
cooking = rng.normal(15, 4, n).clip(min=5)        # minutes in the kitchen
riding  = rng.normal(18, 5, n).clip(min=5)        # minutes on the road
traffic = rng.exponential(3, n)                   # extra delay: usually small, sometimes big

total = cooking + riding + traffic
print(f"average delivery time : {total.mean():.1f} min")
print(f"P(late, over 45 min)  : {np.mean(total > 45):.3f}")
print(f"95% of orders arrive within {np.percentile(total, 95):.1f} min")
Output
average delivery time : 36.0 min
P(late, over 45 min)  : 0.101
95% of orders arrive within 47.8 min

Working this out by hand would be fiddly: the minimum-time clipping and the skewed traffic delay make the formulas messy. The simulation answers in three lines: on average 36 minutes, a 10% chance of being late, and a 47.8-minute promise would be kept 95% of the time. Want to test "what if we cut kitchen time by 3 minutes"? Change one number and run again.

How a language model picks the next word

A language model doesn't "know" the next word. It produces a Categorical distribution over its whole vocabulary (Part 2's softmax), then samples from it. A setting called temperature T reshapes the distribution first: the logits are divided by T before the softmax.

  • Low temperature (below 1) sharpens the distribution: the top word wins more often. As T gets close to 0 it wins every time (this is called greedy decoding). Safe and repetitive.
  • High temperature (above 1) flattens it: unusual words get more chances. Creative, and sometimes nonsensical.
Python
def softmax(z):
    e = np.exp(z - z.max())
    return e / e.sum()

words  = np.array(["pizza", "pasta", "salad", "sushi"])
logits = np.array([2.0, 1.5, 0.5, 0.1])          # a language model's scores for the next word

for T in [0.5, 1.0, 2.0]:
    probs = softmax(logits / T)
    print(f"temperature {T}: " + "  ".join(f"{w} {p:.2f}" for w, p in zip(words, probs)))

picks = rng.choice(words, size=10_000, p=softmax(logits))
print("10,000 samples, T = 1: " + "  ".join(f"{w} {np.mean(picks == w):.2f}" for w in words))
Output
temperature 0.5: pizza 0.69  pasta 0.26  salad 0.03  sushi 0.02
temperature 1.0: pizza 0.51  pasta 0.31  salad 0.11  sushi 0.08
temperature 2.0: pizza 0.38  pasta 0.30  salad 0.18  sushi 0.15
10,000 samples, T = 1: pizza 0.50  pasta 0.31  salad 0.11  sushi 0.07

Temperature and sampling

A model is completing "For dinner tonight, let's have …". Move the temperature, then sample a few completions.

In ML

Randomness is built into deep learning: random weight initialisation, shuffled mini-batches, dropout (randomly switching neurons off), data augmentation (random crops and flips), and sampling text or images from generative models. Monte Carlo methods also estimate quantities that have no formula, from option prices to the uncertainty of a model's prediction.

In business

Simulation is the practical tool for "what if" planning: delivery promises, inventory levels under random demand, revenue ranges for next quarter. It replaces a single, falsely precise forecast with a range and a probability, which is what decision-makers actually need.

Check yourself

1. You simulate 50,000 deliveries and 4,000 are late. Estimate P(late). Answer: 4,000 / 50,000 = 0.08.

2. A chatbot keeps giving the same dull answer. Raise or lower the temperature? Answer: raise it (a little), to flatten the distribution.

12Information theory: entropy, cross-entropy and KL divergence

The last piece connects probability to the losses of deep learning. It starts with a simple question: how surprised should you be?

Intuition · news value

"It's sunny in the desert today" is hardly news: you expected it. "It snowed in the desert" is front-page news. Rare events carry more information than common ones. Information theory measures that surprise: an event with probability p carries −log₂(p) bits of surprise. A coin flip (p = ½) is 1 bit; a 1-in-1,000 event is about 10 bits.

Entropy: the average surprise

Math, decoded
H(P) = −Σ p(x) · log₂ p(x) "average surprise", in bits

High entropy means hard to predict (a fair coin, a 50/50 rainy city). Low entropy means predictable (a desert, a heavily biased coin). Zero entropy means certain.

Python
def entropy(p):
    p = np.asarray(p, dtype=float)
    p = p[p > 0]
    return -np.sum(p * np.log2(p))          # in bits

print("fair coin          :", round(entropy([0.5, 0.5]), 3), "bits")
print("coin with 90% heads:", round(entropy([0.9, 0.1]), 3), "bits")
print("rain 50/50 city    :", round(entropy([0.5, 0.5]), 3), "bits")
print("desert (1% rain)   :", round(entropy([0.01, 0.99]), 3), "bits")
print("fair 6-sided die   :", round(entropy([1/6] * 6), 3), "bits")
Output
fair coin          : 1.0 bits
coin with 90% heads: 0.469 bits
rain 50/50 city    : 1.0 bits
desert (1% rain)   : 0.081 bits
fair 6-sided die   : 2.585 bits

Cross-entropy: surprise when you believe the wrong thing

Suppose the real mix of payments is P (60% UPI, 30% card, 10% cash), but your model believes Q. Cross-entropy H(P, Q) is your average surprise when reality follows P but you expected Q. It can never be lower than H(P), and it equals H(P) only when Q is exactly right.

Math, decoded
H(P, Q) = −Σ p(x) · log q(x) cross-entropy KL(P ‖ Q) = H(P, Q) − H(P) extra surprise caused by believing Q instead of P

KL divergence is the "penalty for being wrong": 0 when Q matches P, larger the worse Q is. It is not a true distance, because KL(P ‖ Q) and KL(Q ‖ P) are usually different.

Python
def cross_entropy(p, q):
    return -np.sum(np.asarray(p) * np.log2(q))

def kl(p, q):
    return cross_entropy(p, q) - entropy(p)

actual   = [0.6, 0.3, 0.1]       # real share of payments: UPI, card, cash
good_mdl = [0.55, 0.33, 0.12]
bad_mdl  = [0.2, 0.3, 0.5]

print(f"entropy of the real mix       : {entropy(actual):.3f} bits")
for name, q in [("good model", good_mdl), ("bad model", bad_mdl)]:
    print(f"{name}: cross-entropy = {cross_entropy(actual, q):.3f}   KL = {kl(actual, q):.3f}")
print(f"KL is not symmetric: KL(P‖Q) = {kl(actual, bad_mdl):.3f}, KL(Q‖P) = {kl(bad_mdl, actual):.3f}")
Output
entropy of the real mix       : 1.295 bits
good model: cross-entropy = 1.303   KL = 0.008
bad model: cross-entropy = 2.014   KL = 0.719
KL is not symmetric: KL(P‖Q) = 0.719, KL(Q‖P) = 0.844

Why this is the classification loss

In training, P is the true label (100% on the correct class) and Q is the model's softmax output. Then H(P) = 0, so cross-entropy and KL divergence are the same thing: −log(probability given to the correct class). That's exactly Part 2's cross-entropy loss and Section 9's negative log-likelihood. Three names, one idea: make the model less surprised by the truth.

One unit detail: this article uses log₂, which measures surprise in bits. ML libraries use the natural log, which measures it in nats (1 nat ≈ 1.44 bits). The numbers differ by that constant factor, so the best model is exactly the same either way.

Information gain: how decision trees choose a question

A decision tree asks the question that removes the most uncertainty. "Information gain" is entropy before the split minus the (weighted) entropy after it.

Python
def node_entropy(churn, stay):
    return entropy([churn / (churn + stay), stay / (churn + stay)])

parent = node_entropy(8, 12)                    # 20 customers: 8 churned, 12 stayed

def info_gain(left, right):                     # left/right = (churn, stay) after the split
    n = sum(left) + sum(right)
    after = sum(left) / n * node_entropy(*left) + sum(right) / n * node_entropy(*right)
    return parent - after

print(f"entropy before any split  : {parent:.3f} bits")
print(f"split on 'filed a complaint': gain = {info_gain((6, 2), (2, 10)):.3f} bits")
print(f"split on 'uses mobile app' : gain = {info_gain((4, 6), (4, 6)):.3f} bits")
Output
entropy before any split  : 0.971 bits
split on 'filed a complaint': gain = 0.256 bits
split on 'uses mobile app' : gain = 0.000 bits

Splitting on "filed a complaint" removes a quarter of a bit of uncertainty. Splitting on "uses the mobile app" removes none, because churn rates are identical in both groups. A decision tree would ask about complaints first.

In ML
  • Cross-entropy: the loss of every classifier and language model ("perplexity" is 2 to the power of the cross-entropy in bits, or e to the power of it in nats).
  • Information gain / entropy: how decision trees and random forests choose splits (criterion="entropy" in scikit-learn).
  • KL divergence: part of the VAE loss; knowledge distillation (a small model learning a big model's probabilities); the penalty in RLHF fine-tuning that keeps a chat model close to its original behaviour; t-SNE.
  • Mutual information: how much knowing one feature reduces uncertainty about the target, used for feature selection.
In business

Entropy is a neat measure of uncertainty in any mix: how concentrated a customer's spending is across categories, how diverse a product portfolio is, how predictable demand is. KL divergence and its close relatives (such as the population stability index, PSI) are common ways to monitor data drift: if this month's mix of customers drifts far (in KL) from the training data, it's time to retrain the model.

Check yourself

1. Which has higher entropy: a fair die or a fair coin? Answer: the die (2.585 bits vs 1 bit); there are more equally likely outcomes.

2. Your model gives the correct class probability 0.25. What is the cross-entropy loss, in bits? Answer: −log₂(0.25) = 2 bits.

3. If KL(P ‖ Q) = 0, what do you know? Answer: Q is exactly equal to P.

Common mistakes and fixes

Mistakes and fixes
  1. Confusing P(A | B) with P(B | A). Write both out as counts in a table before concluding anything.
  2. Ignoring the base rate. For rare events, even a good detector gives mostly false alarms. Do the Bayes count first.
  3. Treating density as probability. For continuous values, only areas (ranges) have probabilities.
  4. Trusting small samples. 3 out of 3 isn't 100%. Use a prior or smoothing, and report how much data you have.
  5. Multiplying many probabilities. The result underflows to 0. Add log-probabilities instead.
  6. Assuming independence without checking. Compare P(A | B) with P(A); if they differ, the events are linked.
  7. Planning for the average. Averages hide the tails. Use the distribution's percentiles for promises and capacity.

Quick recap

  • A probability is a long-run share. Turn percentages into counts of people or orders, and most problems become simple.
  • A few distributions cover everyday ML: Bernoulli (yes/no), Binomial (successes out of n), Categorical (one of k), Poisson (counts) and the Gaussian (bell curve, 68–95–99.7).
  • Expected value is the long-run average; variance is the bumpiness. Averages are steadier by a factor of √n, which is why mini-batches work.
  • "Given" shrinks the crowd. Bayes' theorem flips a conditional and reminds you that rare events produce mostly false alarms.
  • Maximum likelihood picks the settings that best explain the data, and it produces MSE and cross-entropy. Priors add common sense, and they become L2, L1 and smoothing.
  • Sampling answers questions formulas can't, and temperature controls how adventurous a language model is.
  • Entropy measures surprise; cross-entropy is the classification loss; KL divergence is the price of believing the wrong distribution.
