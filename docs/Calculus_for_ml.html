Think back to learning to ride a bicycle. You wobble, you lean too far left, you correct a little to the right, you wobble less. Nobody hands you the formula for balance. You learn it from a loop: try → see how wrong you were → figure out which way to adjust → adjust a little → repeat.

Every machine learning model trains with that exact loop. Part 1 gave us the machinery to make a prediction (a dot product, a matrix multiplication). This article answers the harder question: how does the model find good weights in the first place? The answer is calculus, and specifically one idea from it: the slope. If you know which way is downhill on your error, you know which way to adjust.

We will use one running story the whole way through. A marketing team has 20 months of data: how much they spent on ads and how much they sold. They want a model that predicts sales from ad spend. We'll train that model one weight at a time, then two, then switch to a classification problem, and finish with a neural network that learns a pattern no straight line can.

By the end you will be able to:

  • explain what a loss function is and why training means "minimise the loss",
  • read and compute a derivative, a partial derivative and a gradient, and say what their sign and size mean,
  • write gradient descent from scratch and diagnose a learning rate that is too small, about right or too large,
  • trace backpropagation through a computational graph and check it numerically,
  • know when you can solve for the best weights directly, read the Hessian to tell a minimum from a saddle point, and use Lagrange multipliers for constrained problems,
  • derive softmax + cross-entropy gradients, apply the shape rule to matrix gradients, and explain what L1, L2 and weight decay do to each update,
  • explain vanishing gradients, why Adam is popular, and what loss.backward() in PyTorch actually does.

1Learning = minimising a loss

Intuition · archery in the dark

You're practising archery in a dark room. After each arrow, a friend calls out how far you missed the bullseye. You can't see the target, but that one number is enough to improve: try a slightly different angle, hear whether the number went up or down, adjust. The number your friend calls out is the loss. Your stance and angle are the weights.

Let's make that concrete with the marketing data. Our model is a straight line, sales = w × ad_spend + b. For now we fix the base b = 5.5 and only learn w, the extra sales each ₹1 lakh of ads brings in. To score a guess for w:

  1. predict sales for all 20 months,
  2. compute each month's error (prediction − actual),
  3. square the errors (so misses in either direction count, and big misses count a lot more),
  4. average them. That average is the mean squared error (MSE), our loss.
Math, decoded
L(w) = (1/n) Σᵢ ( w·xᵢ + b − yᵢ )²

"For each month i, take the prediction w·xᵢ + b, subtract the actual sales yᵢ, square it, then average over all n months." Notice the data (x, y) is fixed. The only thing that can change is w. So the loss is a function of the weight, and training means finding the w where it's smallest.

One slider, two views

Move w. Left: the line and its errors (coral). Right: the loss for that w. The curve on the right is every possible w at once.

Python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
ad_spend = rng.uniform(1, 10, 20).round(1)                           # ₹ lakh spent on ads, 20 months
sales = (3.0 * ad_spend + 8 + rng.normal(0, 2, 20)).round(1)         # ₹ lakh of sales those months

def predict(w, b, x):
    return w * x + b                       # the model: a straight line

def mse(w, b):
    errors = predict(w, b, ad_spend) - sales
    return np.mean(errors ** 2)            # mean squared error = the loss

b = 5.5                                    # fix the base for now; we learn only w
for w in [1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 3.24, 4.0, 5.0]:
    print(f"w = {w:4}  →  loss = {mse(w, b):7.2f}")
Output
w =  1.0  →  loss =  228.19
w =  2.0  →  loss =   72.05
w =  3.0  →  loss =    5.24
w = 3.24  →  loss =    2.50
w =  4.0  →  loss =   27.77
w =  5.0  →  loss =  139.64

The loss is high when the line is too flat (w = 1), drops to about 2.5 near w ≈ 3.24, and climbs again when the line is too steep. The curve on the right of the widget is a bowl. Training is finding the bottom of the bowl.

Why squared error, and not something else?

Choosing a loss is choosing what "wrong" means. Squaring makes one big miss far worse than several small ones, and gives a smooth bowl that's easy to slide down. Other losses make other choices: absolute error (MAE) treats all misses proportionally; cross-entropy (Section 10) is the right choice for probabilities.

In business

The loss is where business priorities enter the maths. A retailer that loses far more from stock-outs than from overstock should not use a symmetric loss; an asymmetric one (such as quantile loss) pushes forecasts up. Agreeing on the loss with stakeholders is often more important than choosing the algorithm.

Check yourself

1. Errors for three months are −2, 1 and 3. What is the MSE? Answer: (4 + 1 + 9) / 3 ≈ 4.67.

2. Why is the loss written L(w) and not L(x)? Answer: during training the data is fixed; only the weights change, so the loss is a function of the weights.

2The derivative: the slope of the loss

We can see the bottom of the bowl in the widget because we drew the whole curve. A real model with a million weights can't draw its loss. It only knows the loss at the weights it currently has. So it needs a local clue: if I nudge this weight a little, does the loss go up or down, and how fast? That clue is the derivative.

Intuition · the speedometer and the nudge test

Your car's trip meter tells you how far you've gone. The speedometer tells you something different: how fast that distance is changing right now. The derivative is the speedometer of a function.

You can measure it with a nudge test: move w a tiny bit to the right and see how much the loss changes. Divide the change in loss by the size of the nudge. That ratio is the slope.

Math, decoded
L′(w) ≈ [ L(w + h) − L(w − h) ] / (2h) with h tiny, e.g. 0.0001

"Nudge a hair to the right and a hair to the left; rise over run." Textbooks write this with a limit (h → 0), which only means "make the nudge as small as you like". The two things that matter:

SlopeMeansTo reduce loss
NegativeLoss goes down as w increasesIncrease w
PositiveLoss goes up as w increasesDecrease w
ZeroFlat: at the bottom (or a flat spot)Stop
Large sizeSteep: far from the bottom, usuallyBig moves help

In one line: always move w in the opposite direction to the slope.

The tangent line

The dashed line touches the curve at w and has the same slope. Watch the sign flip as you cross the bottom.

Python
def loss_w(w):
    return mse(w, b)                        # loss as a function of the one weight we are tuning

def nudge_slope(f, w, h=1e-4):
    return (f(w + h) - f(w - h)) / (2 * h)  # nudge a tiny bit each way, see how much f moves

for w in [1.0, 3.24, 5.0]:
    print(f"w = {w:4}:  slope ≈ {nudge_slope(loss_w, w):8.2f}")
Output
w =  1.0:  slope ≈  -200.81
w = 3.24:  slope ≈    -0.70
w =  5.0:  slope ≈   156.54

Left of the bottom the slope is strongly negative (increase w). Near the bottom it's almost zero. Right of the bottom it's positive (decrease w). The nudge test works for any function, but it costs two loss evaluations per weight. With a million weights that's two million passes over the data for a single step. We need formulas.

By hand · the derivative of our loss
L(w) = (1/n) Σ ( w·xᵢ + b − yᵢ )² Call the error eᵢ = w·xᵢ + b − yᵢ. Then L = (1/n) Σ eᵢ² slope of eᵢ² with respect to eᵢ : 2·eᵢ (power rule) slope of eᵢ with respect to w : xᵢ (c·x rule; b and yᵢ are constants) multiply them (Section 8 explains why): 2·eᵢ·xᵢ L′(w) = (1/n) Σ 2·eᵢ·xᵢ = "average of 2 × error × input"

Read the result: if predictions are too high (errors positive) for months with big ad spend, the slope is positive, so lower w. That's exactly what common sense says.

Python
def dloss_dw(w, b=5.5):
    errors = predict(w, b, ad_spend) - sales
    return np.mean(2 * errors * ad_spend)   # the formula we derived by hand

for w in [1.0, 3.24, 5.0]:
    print(f"w = {w:4}:  formula = {dloss_dw(w):8.2f}   nudge = {nudge_slope(loss_w, w):8.2f}")
Output
w =  1.0:  formula =  -200.81   nudge =  -200.81
w = 3.24:  formula =    -0.70   nudge =    -0.70
w =  5.0:  formula =   156.54   nudge =   156.54

The formula and the nudge test agree to every digit shown. The formula costs one pass over the data and gives the slope for w directly. Comparing a formula against the nudge test is called a gradient check, and it is how you catch bugs in hand-written gradients (we'll do it again for a neural network in Section 12).

In ML

Every trainable model needs the slope of its loss with respect to each weight. Linear and logistic regression, neural networks and gradient boosting all use the rules in that table. Gradient boosting (XGBoost, LightGBM) even uses the second derivative, which we meet next.

In business

Economists have used derivatives for a long time under the name marginal analysis. "What extra sales does the next ₹1 lakh of ads bring?" is a derivative. So is "how much does demand drop if we raise the price by ₹1?" (price elasticity). Any time someone says "at the margin", they mean a slope.

Check yourself

1. At the current w, the slope of the loss is +40. Should you increase or decrease w? Answer: decrease. Positive slope means the loss rises as w rises.

2. What is the derivative of 5w² + 3w + 7? Answer: 10w + 3.

3Gradient descent with one weight

Intuition · walking downhill in fog

You're on a hillside in thick fog and want to reach the valley floor. You can't see the valley, but you can feel the ground under your feet. Feel which way it tilts, take a step downhill, feel again, step again. Steep ground: you're probably far from the bottom. Flat ground: you've arrived.

Math, decoded · the update rule
w_new = w_old − α × L′(w_old)

"Take the slope, scale it by the learning rate α, and step the opposite way." The minus sign does the "opposite" for you: a negative slope makes −α × slope positive, so w increases, and vice versa. Steep slopes produce big steps automatically, and the steps shrink as you approach the flat bottom.

Python
w = 0.0          # start from a bad guess
lr = 0.01        # learning rate: how big each step is

for step in range(1, 31):
    grad = dloss_dw(w)
    w = w - lr * grad                       # step AGAINST the slope
    if step in (1, 2, 3, 5, 10, 20, 30):
        print(f"step {step:2d}: w = {w:6.3f}   loss = {loss_w(w):7.3f}   slope = {dloss_dw(w):8.3f}")
Output
step  1: w =  2.901   loss =   7.855   slope =  -30.938
step  2: w =  3.211   loss =   2.559   slope =   -3.299
step  3: w =  3.244   loss =   2.499   slope =   -0.352
step  5: w =  3.248   loss =   2.498   slope =   -0.004
step 10: w =  3.248   loss =   2.498   slope =   -0.000
step 20: w =  3.248   loss =   2.498   slope =    0.000
step 30: w =  3.248   loss =   2.498   slope =    0.000

Starting from w = 0, three steps get us very close to the answer, and the slope shrinks towards zero as we arrive. That shrinking slope is also how you know when to stop: when the slope (or the improvement in loss) is below a small tolerance, or when a validation metric stops improving.

The learning rate: the one setting that makes or breaks training

Intuition · seasoning soup

The soup needs salt. A pinch at a time, tasting in between, gets you there. A grain at a time takes all evening. Tipping the shaker in at once overshoots so badly you can never recover. The learning rate is the size of the pinch.

Gradient descent, step by step

Pick a learning rate and a starting point, then press Step or Run. Try 0.002, 0.01, 0.02 and 0.023.

Python
def run(lr, steps=25, w=0.0):
    for _ in range(steps):
        w = w - lr * dloss_dw(w)
    return w, loss_w(w)

for lr in [0.001, 0.01, 0.02, 0.03]:
    w_end, loss_end = run(lr)
    print(f"lr = {lr:<6} → w = {w_end:12.3f}   loss = {loss_end:14.2f}")
Output
lr = 0.001  → w =        2.935   loss =           6.87
lr = 0.01   → w =        3.248   loss =           2.50
lr = 0.02   → w =        3.256   loss =           2.50
lr = 0.03   → w =  1396471.721   loss = 87109122655643.75

Too small (0.001): after 25 steps we still haven't arrived. About right (0.01 to 0.02): we land on 3.25. Too big (0.03): every step overshoots further than the last, and the weight explodes to over a million. In real training this shows up as a loss that becomes nan or inf.

Why 0.03 explodes: curvature and the second derivative

The second derivative is the slope of the slope. It measures curvature: how quickly the ground steepens. A narrow, sharply curved bowl punishes big steps because you jump straight past the bottom and up the other side. For a bowl-shaped loss there's a neat rule: the learning rate must stay below 2 ÷ curvature.

Python
curvature = 2 * np.mean(ad_spend ** 2)     # second derivative of the loss with respect to w
print(f"curvature L''(w) = {curvature:.1f}")
print(f"largest safe learning rate ≈ 2 / curvature = {2 / curvature:.4f}")
Output
curvature L''(w) = 89.3
largest safe learning rate ≈ 2 / curvature = 0.0224

0.02 is just under the limit (it zig-zags but settles), and 0.03 is over it (it zig-zags outward forever). You can watch both in the widget above. In practice you don't compute curvature for a deep network; you try a few learning rates spaced by about 3× (0.001, 0.003, 0.01, 0.03…) and keep the largest one that trains stably.

In ML

Learning rate is the first hyperparameter to tune for any neural network. Second-derivative information is used directly by Newton's method and L-BFGS, and by XGBoost and LightGBM, which use first and second derivatives of the loss when choosing each tree's splits and leaf values.

In business

Training time is compute cost. A learning rate that's too small can multiply your cloud bill; one that's too large wastes a run that has to be restarted. Teams usually run a quick learning-rate sweep on a small sample of the data before committing to a full training job.

Check yourself

1. w = 2, slope = −30, α = 0.01. What is the new w? Answer: 2 − 0.01 × (−30) = 2.3.

2. Your training loss jumps to nan after a few steps. What's the first thing to try? Answer: lower the learning rate (and check for unscaled features).

4Two weights: partial derivatives and the gradient

Now let the model learn b too. With two weights the loss is no longer a curve; it's a surface, a landscape over the (w, b) plane. We draw it from above as a contour map, like a hiking map: each ring connects points with the same loss, and the bottom of the valley is in the middle.

Intuition · one knob at a time

A sound engineer has two knobs, bass and treble. To understand each one, she holds treble still and turns only bass, listening to the change. Then the other way round. A partial derivative is that: the slope along one weight while every other weight is frozen. The gradient collects all the partial derivatives into one vector, one entry per knob.

Math, decoded
∂L/∂w = (1/n) Σ 2·eᵢ·xᵢ (b treated as a constant) ∂L/∂b = (1/n) Σ 2·eᵢ·1 (w treated as a constant) ∇L = [ ∂L/∂w , ∂L/∂b ] the gradient: a vector (Part 1!) [w, b] ← [w, b] − α ∇L same update rule, now for a vector

Two facts make gradient descent work. The gradient points in the direction of steepest uphill, so its negative is steepest downhill. And it is always perpendicular to the contour line you're standing on: the fastest way off a level path is straight across it.

Python
def gradient(w, b, x=ad_spend):
    errors = predict(w, b, x) - sales
    dL_dw = np.mean(2 * errors * x)         # slope along w, holding b still
    dL_db = np.mean(2 * errors)             # slope along b, holding w still
    return np.array([dL_dw, dL_db])

def descend(lr, x=ad_spend, tol=1e-3, max_steps=20_000):
    params = np.array([0.0, 0.0])           # [w, b]
    for step in range(1, max_steps + 1):
        params = params - lr * gradient(*params, x=x)
        if np.linalg.norm(gradient(*params, x=x)) < tol:   # gradient ≈ 0 → at the bottom
            return params, step
    return params, max_steps

params, steps = descend(lr=0.02)
print(f"raw ad spend    : w = {params[0]:.3f}, b = {params[1]:.3f}  after {steps} steps")
print("numpy's exact fit:", np.polyfit(ad_spend, sales, 1).round(3))
Output
raw ad spend    : w = 3.241, b = 5.548  after 1285 steps
numpy's exact fit: [3.241 5.552]

We got the same answer as NumPy's exact fit, but it took 1,285 steps. Why so many for two weights? The widget shows it.

The loss landscape from above

Click anywhere to start gradient descent from that point. Then switch to scaled ad spend and try again.

With raw ad spend, the valley is a long, narrow trench. The gradient points mostly across the trench (the steep direction), so gradient descent zig-zags from wall to wall and creeps along the floor. The learning rate can't be raised to speed things up, because the steep direction would then explode (Section 3's 2 ÷ curvature limit).

Standardising the feature (mean 0, spread 1) turns the trench into a round bowl. Now the gradient points straight at the bottom.

Python
mu, sd = ad_spend.mean(), ad_spend.std()
ad_scaled = (ad_spend - mu) / sd            # standardise: mean 0, spread 1

params_s, steps_s = descend(lr=0.1, x=ad_scaled)
w_raw = params_s[0] / sd                    # convert back to "per ₹ lakh" units
b_raw = params_s[1] - params_s[0] * mu / sd
print(f"scaled ad spend : w = {w_raw:.3f}, b = {b_raw:.3f}  after {steps_s} steps")
Output
scaled ad spend : w = 3.241, b = 5.551  after 49 steps

49 steps instead of 1,285, for exactly the same model. This is the calculus reason behind the advice from Part 1 to scale your features. It's also one of the reasons deep networks use batch normalisation and layer normalisation: keeping each layer's inputs well-scaled keeps the landscape closer to round.

In ML

A neural network's gradient has one entry per weight, often millions or billions. It's still "a vector of partial derivatives", and the update is still w ← w − α∇L. Everything in later sections is either a faster way to compute ∇L (backprop) or a smarter way to use it (optimisers).

In business

Partial derivatives answer "what if we change only this?" questions: the effect of price on revenue with the ad budget held fixed, or of discount depth on margin with volume held fixed. Sensitivity tables in financial models are partial derivatives laid out in a grid.

Check yourself

1. ∇L = [4, −1]. Which way should w and b move? Answer: w down, b up (opposite signs to the gradient).

2. Gradient descent is zig-zagging and slow. Name two fixes. Answer: scale the features; use momentum or an adaptive optimiser (Section 15).

5Setting the gradient to zero: closed-form answers

Gradient descent walks down to the bottom of the bowl one step at a time. But at the bottom the slope is exactly zero in every direction. So for some losses we can skip the walk: write down "gradient = 0" and solve the equation directly. This gives a closed-form solution, and it explains where several famous formulas come from.

Intuition · the flat spot is the answer

Picture a bowl. Everywhere on its sides the floor tilts. At exactly one place, the very bottom, it's flat. "Find the lowest point" and "find where it's flat" are the same question for a bowl, and the second one is sometimes just algebra.

By hand · one weight
L′(w) = (1/n) Σ 2·(w·xᵢ + b − yᵢ)·xᵢ = 0 Divide by 2, split the sum: w·Σxᵢ² = Σ(yᵢ − b)·xᵢ w = Σ(yᵢ − b)·xᵢ / Σxᵢ²
Python
# Set dL/dw = 0 and solve for w (b fixed at 5.5):
#   mean(2·(w·x + b − y)·x) = 0   →   w = mean((y − b)·x) / mean(x²)
w_exact = np.mean((sales - 5.5) * ad_spend) / np.mean(ad_spend ** 2)
print(f"w from 'slope = 0'  : {w_exact:.4f}")
print(f"slope at that w     : {dloss_dw(w_exact):.1e}")
Output
w from 'slope = 0'  : 3.2478
slope at that w     : 6.5e-15

Gradient descent in Section 3 settled on 3.248. Setting the slope to zero gives 3.2478 in one line, and the slope there is zero up to rounding.

Two weights: the normal equation from Part 1

Do the same with both w and b: set both partial derivatives to zero. Written with matrices (stack ad spend and a column of 1s into X, and the two weights into θ), the two equations become one:

Math, decoded
∇L(θ) = (2/n) Xᵀ(Xθ − y) = 0 ⟺ XᵀX θ = Xᵀy ⟺ θ = (XᵀX)⁻¹ Xᵀy

That last form is the normal equation you met in Part 1 (Section 9). It wasn't magic: it is simply "the gradient of the squared error equals zero", rearranged.

Python
X = np.column_stack([ad_spend, np.ones_like(ad_spend)])   # columns: ad spend, 1 (carries b)
theta = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ sales)              # ∇L = 0  ⟺  XᵀX θ = Xᵀy
print("closed-form [w, b]:", theta.round(3))
print("gradient there    :", gradient(*theta).round(8))
Output
closed-form [w, b]: [3.241 5.552]
gradient there    : [-0.  0.]

Same answer as the 1,285 steps of gradient descent in Section 4, with no learning rate and no iterations. So why does anyone use gradient descent? Because a closed form exists only for a few losses (squared error with a linear model is the main one), and even then solving XᵀX θ = Xᵀy gets expensive with very many features. Logistic regression and every neural network have no closed form, so iterative methods are the only option.

What your loss is secretly predicting

Setting the derivative to zero also reveals what a loss function "wants". Suppose you must forecast tomorrow's orders with a single number c. Minimise squared error, (1/n)Σ(yᵢ − c)²: its derivative is −(2/n)Σ(yᵢ − c), which is zero when c is the mean. Minimise absolute error instead and the answer is the median.

Python
daily_orders = np.array([12, 15, 14, 13, 90])   # one bulk order on the last day

candidates = np.linspace(0, 100, 10001)          # try every forecast from 0 to 100
best_sq  = candidates[np.argmin([np.mean((daily_orders - c) ** 2) for c in candidates])]
best_abs = candidates[np.argmin([np.mean(np.abs(daily_orders - c)) for c in candidates])]

print(f"minimises absolute error: {best_abs:.2f}   (median = {np.median(daily_orders):.2f})")

One bulk order drags the squared-error forecast to 28.8, while the absolute-error forecast stays at a typical day's 14. Neither is wrong; they answer different questions. This is the calculus behind the advice in Section 1 that choosing a loss is choosing what "wrong" means.

In ML

Closed forms you will meet: ordinary least squares and Ridge regression (the normal equation plus λI), the mean and covariance estimates in Gaussian models, and the leaf values of gradient-boosted trees, which XGBoost computes with a one-step formula built from gradients and second derivatives (Section 6). In probability terms, minimising squared error is the same as maximum likelihood under Gaussian noise, and minimising cross-entropy is maximum likelihood for yes/no outcomes; both are found by setting a derivative to zero.

In business

When a forecast feeds a decision, the loss decides which "average" you get. Staffing for typical days? The median-type forecast (absolute error). Budgeting total monthly volume? The mean-type forecast (squared error), because totals are sums and sums follow the mean.

6Curvature in many dimensions: the Hessian, convexity and saddle points

Section 3 used the second derivative of a one-weight loss to explain why a learning rate can be too big. With many weights, curvature becomes a matrix, and that matrix answers three questions every practitioner runs into: is this a true minimum? will gradient descent find the best one? and how stretched is the valley?

The local picture: Taylor's approximation

Intuition · close up, every curve looks simple

Zoom far enough into any smooth curve and it looks like a straight line. Zoom a little less and it looks like a parabola. Gradient descent trusts the straight-line view (slope only); second-order methods trust the parabola view (slope plus curvature).

Math, decoded
L(w + Δ) ≈ L(w) + L′(w)·Δ + ½·L″(w)·Δ² one weight L(θ + Δ) ≈ L(θ) + ∇L · Δ + ½ Δᵀ H Δ many weights

The middle term, ∇L · Δ, is a dot product (Part 1, Section 4). It is most negative when Δ points exactly opposite to ∇L. That is the precise reason the negative gradient is the steepest way down. H, the Hessian, is the matrix of all second derivatives: entry (i, j) is ∂²L/∂θᵢ∂θⱼ. It is symmetric.

Python
w0, step = 2.0, 0.3
actual = loss_w(w0 + step)
first  = loss_w(w0) + dloss_dw(w0) * step              # straight-line guess (slope only)
second = first + 0.5 * curvature * step ** 2            # add the curvature term

print(f"actual loss     : {actual:.4f}")
print(f"1st-order guess : {first:.4f}")
print(f"2nd-order guess : {second:.4f}")
Output
actual loss     : 42.6245
1st-order guess : 38.6043
2nd-order guess : 42.6245

The slope-only guess is 4 units off; adding the curvature term makes it exact, because a squared-error loss is itself a perfect parabola in w.

For squared error with a linear model the Hessian is (2/n) XᵀX, the same matrix from the normal equation. Its eigenvalues (Part 1, Section 11) are the curvatures along the valley's two natural directions.

Saddle points

Intuition · a horse's saddle, or a mountain pass

On a mountain pass the ground is flat where you stand, yet it's a low point along the ridge and a high point along the trail. The gradient is zero, but it is neither a minimum nor a maximum. The Hessian tells them apart: all eigenvalues positive means a minimum, all negative a maximum, mixed signs a saddle.

Python
def classify(H):
    ev = np.linalg.eigvalsh(H)
    if np.all(ev > 0): return ev, "minimum (bowl)"
    if np.all(ev < 0): return ev, "maximum (hilltop)"
    return ev, "saddle point"

for name, Hm in [("x² + y²", [[2, 0], [0, 2]]),
                 ("x² − y²", [[2, 0], [0, -2]]),
                 ("−x² − y²", [[-2, 0], [0, -2]])]:
    ev, kind = classify(np.array(Hm, dtype=float))
    print(f"{name:9s} Hessian eigenvalues {ev} → {kind}")

In a network with millions of weights, a flat point would need every one of millions of curvatures to be positive to count as a minimum. It's far more likely that some are positive and some negative. Research on deep networks suggests that saddle points and long flat regions, rather than bad local minima, are the main things that slow training down. The noise of mini-batches and the inertia of momentum (Section 15) help push the weights off them.

Newton's method: use the curvature to jump

If the loss is (close to) a parabola, the Taylor formula tells you exactly where its bottom is: step Δ = −H⁻¹∇L. That is Newton's method. On our bowl-shaped loss it lands on the answer in a single step, no learning rate needed:

Python
theta = np.zeros(2)                                    # start at w = 0, b = 0

print("gradient there       :", gradient(*theta).round(8))
Output
after one Newton step: [3.241 5.552]
gradient there       : [ 0. -0.]

The catch is cost. A network with a million weights has a Hessian with a trillion entries, far too many to store or invert. Deep learning therefore sticks to first-order methods, while adaptive optimisers such as RMSProp and Adam (Section 15) borrow a cheap, diagonal hint of curvature. Smaller models use second-order ideas directly: scikit-learn's default LogisticRegression solver is L-BFGS, a quasi-Newton method, and XGBoost and LightGBM use second derivatives of the loss to set each tree's leaf values.

In ML

Convexity is why linear and logistic regression always give the same answer however you start, and why neural networks don't. The Hessian's eigenvalues explain the learning-rate limit (below 2 ÷ largest curvature), why badly scaled features slow training, and why optimisers need ways to escape saddle points.

2. Why isn't Newton's method used to train large neural networks? Answer: the Hessian has (number of weights)² entries; storing and inverting it is impossible at that scale.

3. A loss is convex. Can gradient descent get stuck in a bad local minimum? Answer: no. Every local minimum of a convex function is the global minimum.

7Optimising with constraints: Lagrange multipliers

So far the weights could move anywhere. Many real problems add a rule: the budget must add up to ₹100 lakh, a direction must have length 1, the probabilities must sum to 1. Optimising under such a rule is constrained optimisation, and the classic calculus tool for it is the Lagrange multiplier.

Intuition · equal returns at the margin

You split a fixed ad budget between two channels. If one more rupee earns more on channel A than on channel B, move a rupee from B to A, and you're better off. Keep doing it until the next rupee earns the same on both. At the best split, the marginal returns are equal. A Lagrange multiplier is that common marginal return.

Math, decoded
maximise f(θ) subject to (s.t.) g(θ) = c at the best point: ∇f(θ) = λ · ∇g(θ)

"At the optimum, the direction that would improve f points straight into the wall of the constraint." Any sideways improvement would still be available otherwise. λ (lambda) is the Lagrange multiplier; it tells you how much the best value would rise if the constraint were loosened by one unit. For a budget, that is the return on one extra rupee.

By hand · the budget split
revenue f(a, b) = 10√a + 6√b constraint g(a, b) = a + b = 100 ∂f/∂a = 5/√a ∂f/∂b = 3/√b ∇g = [1, 1] ∇f = λ∇g → 5/√a = λ = 3/√b → √a/√b = 5/3 → a/b = 25/9 a + b = 100 → a = 100 × 25/34 ≈ 73.53, b ≈ 26.47
Python
budget = 100                                    # ₹ lakh to split between two channels
a = np.linspace(0.01, budget - 0.01, 100_001)   # spend on channel A; the rest goes to B
revenue = 10 * np.sqrt(a) + 6 * np.sqrt(budget - a)

best_a = a[np.argmax(revenue)]
print(f"best split by brute force : A = {best_a:.2f}, B = {budget - best_a:.2f}")

a_star = budget * 25 / 34                       # from the Lagrange condition (worked above)
print(f"Lagrange answer           : A = {a_star:.2f}, B = {budget - a_star:.2f}")
print(f"marginal return A: {5 / np.sqrt(a_star):.4f}   B: {3 / np.sqrt(budget - a_star):.4f}")
Output

marginal return A: 0.5831   B: 0.5831

Brute force over 100,001 splits agrees with the two-line Lagrange answer, and at that split one more rupee earns 0.583 on either channel. That 0.583 is λ.

Split the budget

Move spend between the channels. The best split is where the two marginal-return curves cross.

PCA is a constrained optimisation

Part 1 found principal components as eigenvectors. Here's why. PCA wants the direction u that maximises the spread uᵀCu, but u must have length 1 (otherwise you could inflate the spread just by making u longer). The Lagrange condition is 2Cu = λ·2u, which is exactly Cu = λu: u must be an eigenvector, and the spread it achieves is its eigenvalue.

Python
C = np.array([[1.0, 0.854],
              [0.854, 1.0]])                      # covariance from Part 1's PCA example

angles = np.linspace(0, np.pi, 18_001)
U = np.column_stack([np.cos(angles), np.sin(angles)])   # every unit-length direction
spread = np.sum((U @ C) * U, axis=1)                    # uᵀ C u for each one
best = U[np.argmax(spread)]

vals, vecs = np.linalg.eigh(C)
print("best direction by search :", best.round(4), " spread =", spread.max().round(4))
print("top eigenvector of C     :", np.abs(vecs[:, 1]).round(4), " eigenvalue =", vals[1].round(4))
Output
best direction by search : [0.7071 0.7071]  spread = 1.854
top eigenvector of C     : [0.7071 0.7071]  eigenvalue = 1.854
In ML
  • PCA: maximise variance subject to unit length gives the eigenvector equation.
  • Support vector machines: maximise the margin subject to every point being on the correct side. The Lagrange multipliers become the famous "dual" variables, and points with non-zero multipliers are the support vectors.
  • Regularisation: "keep the weights small" can be written as a constraint (‖w‖ ≤ t) or as a penalty (+λ‖w‖²). Lagrange multipliers show the two are equivalent, with λ linking them (Section 14).
  • Softmax arises as the solution of a constrained problem where probabilities must sum to 1 (maximum entropy).
In business

Budget allocation across channels, regions or products is the textbook case: at the optimum, the marginal return per rupee is equal everywhere, and λ is the value of one more rupee of budget (economists call it the shadow price). If λ is higher than your cost of capital, it's worth asking for a bigger budget.

2. Why does PCA need the constraint ‖u‖ = 1? Answer: without it, doubling u quadruples uᵀCu, so the "maximum" would be infinite.

8The chain rule and computational graphs

Real models are chains: inputs go into a weighted sum, the sum goes into an activation, the activation goes into a loss. To get the slope of the loss with respect to a weight at the start of the chain, we need the chain rule.

Intuition · gears

Gear A turns gear B, which turns gear C. If B spins 3 times for every turn of A, and C spins 4 times for every turn of B, then C spins 3 × 4 = 12 times for every turn of A. Rates along a chain multiply. That is the entire chain rule.

Math, decoded
if y = f(u) and u = g(x), then dy/dx = dy/du × du/dx

"Slope of the outside (at the inside value) times slope of the inside." It can be as long as you need: dL/dw = dL/dp × dp/dz × dz/dw.

By hand
y = (3x + 2)⁴ inside u = 3x + 2 du/dx = 3 outside y = u⁴ dy/du = 4u³ dy/dx = 4(3x + 2)³ × 3 at x = 0: 4 × 8 × 3 = 96
Python
# y = (3x + 2)^4.  Inside: u = 3x + 2 (du/dx = 3).  Outside: y = u^4 (dy/du = 4u^3)
def y(x):           return (3 * x + 2) ** 4
def dy_dx_chain(x): return 4 * (3 * x + 2) ** 3 * 3    # outer slope × inner slope

for x in [-1.0, 0.0, 1.0]:
    print(f"x = {x:4}:  chain rule = {dy_dx_chain(x):8.1f}   nudge = {nudge_slope(y, x):8.1f}")
Output

Computational graphs: the chain rule as a picture

Draw every small operation as a box and connect them in order. That drawing is a computational graph. Here is one neuron classifying one example (input x = 2, true label 1), using the sigmoid and the "log loss" you'll meet properly in Section 10.

Training on this graph happens in two sweeps:

  • Forward pass (left to right): compute each box's value and remember it.
  • Backward pass (right to left): start with "the slope of L with respect to itself is 1". At each box, multiply the incoming slope by that box's local slope and pass it left. When you reach a weight, you have its gradient.

Forward, then backward

Step through. Values (teal) flow right; gradients (coral) flow left. Change w or b and step again.

true label y = 1
Python
def sigmoid(z):
    return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))

x, y_true = 2.0, 1.0          # one training example (label 1)
w, b = 0.5, -0.5              # current weights

# ---- forward: compute and remember every intermediate value ----
z = w * x + b                 # 0.5
p = sigmoid(z)                # predicted probability
L = -np.log(p)                # loss when the true label is 1

# ---- backward: local slopes, multiplied right to left ----
dL_dp = -1 / p                # slope of -log(p)
dp_dz = p * (1 - p)           # slope of the sigmoid
dL_dz = dL_dp * dp_dz         # chain rule
dL_dw = dL_dz * x             # z = w·x + b  → dz/dw = x
dL_db = dL_dz * 1             #              → dz/db = 1

print(f"forward : z = {z:.4f}, p = {p:.4f}, L = {L:.4f}")
print(f"backward: dL/dz = {dL_dz:.4f}  (equals p − y = {p - y_true:.4f})")
print(f"          dL/dw = {dL_dw:.4f},  dL/db = {dL_db:.4f}")
Output

Look at the second output line. The two messy local slopes, −1/p from the log and p(1 − p) from the sigmoid, multiply out to simply p − y: predicted probability minus true label. That tidy result is no accident, and it's the main reason sigmoid and log loss are always used together.

In ML

The computational graph is how every deep-learning framework sees your model. PyTorch, TensorFlow and JAX record the forward pass as a graph, then run the backward sweep automatically. Section 17 builds a tiny version of that engine. Backpropagation is nothing more than the chain rule applied to this graph, reusing the values saved on the way forward.

In business

Business metrics are chains too: price → demand → revenue → profit. If raising price by ₹1 cuts demand by 50 units, and each unit is worth ₹200 of profit, the effect of price on profit through that path is −50 × 200 = −₹10,000. That is the chain rule. It's what driver-tree models in FP&A teams compute.

9Gradients for vectors and matrices: Jacobians and the shape rule

Section 8's graph pushed single numbers backwards. Real layers take a vector in and give a vector out, and their weights are matrices. This section gives you the two rules that turn the chain rule into the matrix formulas of backpropagation. With them, you can check any backprop code by looking at shapes alone.

Intuition · a sensitivity table

A layer with 3 inputs and 2 outputs has 3 × 2 = 6 "if I nudge this input, how much does that output move?" numbers. Lay them out in a table, one row per output and one column per input, and you have the Jacobian. It is simply the derivative of a vector function, written as a matrix.

Math, decoded
z = f(x), x has n entries, z has m entries J = ∂z/∂x is an (m × n) matrix, Jᵢⱼ = ∂zᵢ/∂xⱼ

For a dense layer z = x @ W (W of shape inputs × outputs), the Jacobian with respect to x is just Wᵀ. The chain rule for vectors multiplies Jacobians the way Section 8 multiplied single slopes.

Python
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = rng.normal(size=3)               # one example with 3 features
W = rng.normal(size=(3, 2))          # layer: 3 inputs → 2 outputs
z = x @ W                            # (2,)

# Jacobian dz/dx by nudging each input: shape (outputs, inputs) = (2, 3)
J = np.zeros((2, 3))
for i in range(3):
    e = np.zeros(3); e[i] = 1e-6
    J[:, i] = ((x + e) @ W - (x - e) @ W) / 2e-6
print("Jacobian dz/dx equals Wᵀ?", np.allclose(J, W.T))
Output
Jacobian dz/dx equals Wᵀ? True

Backprop never builds the Jacobian

Jacobians can be enormous: a layer mapping 4,096 numbers to 4,096 numbers has a 16.8-million-entry Jacobian per example. Backprop avoids building it, because the loss at the end is a single number. What arrives from later layers is a vector of gradients g = ∂L/∂z, the same shape as z. Each layer only needs to compute:

The two formulas of every dense layer (z = x @ W)
∂L/∂x = W @ g send the gradient back to the inputs (a "vector–Jacobian product") ∂L/∂W = outer(x, g) gradient for the weights (an outer product, Part 1 §10)
Python
g = np.array([0.5, -2.0])            # upstream gradient dL/dz arriving from later layers

dL_dx = W @ g                        # vector–Jacobian product: (3,2) @ (2,) → (3,)  same shape as x
dL_dW = np.outer(x, g)               # outer product (Part 1): (3,) ⊗ (2,) → (3,2)  same shape as W
print("dL/dx shape:", dL_dx.shape, " dL/dW shape:", dL_dW.shape, " W shape:", W.shape)

# Check dL/dW numerically, using L = g · z
L = lambda Wm: g @ (x @ Wm)
num = np.zeros_like(W)
for i in range(3):
    for j in range(2):
        E = np.zeros_like(W); E[i, j] = 1e-6
        num[i, j] = (L(W + E) - L(W - E)) / 2e-6
print("outer-product formula matches nudging?", np.allclose(dL_dW, num))

The shape rule

The rule that catches most backprop bugs

The gradient of the loss with respect to anything has exactly the same shape as that thing. W is (3, 2), so ∂L/∂W must be (3, 2). x is (3,), so ∂L/∂x is (3,). If your hand-written gradient has a different shape, or only matches after a mysterious transpose, it's wrong. In PyTorch, w.grad.shape == w.shape always.

For a batch, each example contributes its own outer product, and the gradient is their sum. Stacking the examples in rows turns that sum into one matrix multiplication, which is the dW = Xᵀ @ dZ you'll see in Section 12:

Python
Xb = rng.normal(size=(64, 3))        # a batch of 64 examples

batch_grad = Xb.T @ G                                        # (3,64) @ (64,2) → (3,2)
summed = sum(np.outer(Xb[k], G[k]) for k in range(64))       # add up 64 outer products
print("Xᵀ @ G equals the sum of per-example outer products?", np.allclose(batch_grad, summed))

10Logistic regression and cross-entropy

Our marketing model predicted a number. Many business questions need a probability instead: will this borrower default? will this customer churn? Logistic regression takes the familiar weighted sum z = w·x + b and squashes it through the sigmoid, p = σ(z), into a value between 0 and 1.

Why not just use squared error?

Intuition · a confident wrong answer deserves a loud correction

A student who answers "definitely B" when the answer is A needs a strong correction, more than one who said "maybe A or B". A good loss should give a big slope when the model is confidently wrong. Squared error on top of a sigmoid does the opposite: when the sigmoid is saturated (p near 0 or 1), its slope is almost zero, and that tiny number gets multiplied into the gradient.

Math, decoded · binary cross-entropy (log loss)
L = −[ y·log(p) + (1 − y)·log(1 − p) ] averaged over examples

Only one of the two terms is "on" for each example. If y = 1, the loss is −log(p): near 0 when p ≈ 1, huge when p ≈ 0. If y = 0, it's −log(1 − p). Combined with the sigmoid, its slope with respect to z is just p − y (Section 8).

Python
y_true = 1.0
for z in [-6.0, -2.0, 0.0, 2.0]:
    p = sigmoid(z)
    grad_mse = 2 * (p - y_true) * p * (1 - p)     # d/dz of (p − y)²
    grad_ce  = p - y_true                          # d/dz of cross-entropy
    print(f"z = {z:5}  p = {p:.4f}   MSE grad = {grad_mse:8.4f}   cross-entropy grad = {grad_ce:8.4f}")
Output

Training a loan-default model from scratch

A lender has 400 past loans with two standardised features, credit score and debt-to-income ratio, and whether each loan defaulted. The whole training loop is the gradient descent you already know, using p − y as the error signal and Part 1's matrix multiplication to handle all 400 loans at once.

Python
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
n = 400
credit = rng.normal(0, 1, n)                  # standardised credit score
dti    = rng.normal(0, 1, n)                  # standardised debt-to-income ratio
true_logit = -1.0 - 1.8 * credit + 1.4 * dti
default = (rng.uniform(size=n) < sigmoid(true_logit)).astype(float)   # 1 = defaulted

X = np.column_stack([credit, dti])
w, b, lr = np.zeros(2), 0.0, 0.5

for epoch in range(1, 301):
    p = sigmoid(X @ w + b)                     # forward: probabilities for all 400
    grad_z = (p - default) / n                 # the famous p − y (averaged)

        acc = np.mean((p > 0.5) == default)
        print(f"epoch {epoch:3d}: loss = {loss:.4f}  accuracy = {acc:.1%}  w = {w.round(2)}  b = {b:.2f}")

11Softmax and multi-class cross-entropy

Logistic regression answers yes/no questions. Most real classifiers choose between many options: which of 3 actions a shopper takes, which of 1,000 objects is in a photo, which of 50,000 tokens comes next in a sentence. The tool for all of them is softmax with cross-entropy, and its gradient turns out to be the same p − y you already know.

Intuition · turning scores into shares

A model gives each option a raw score (a logit), which can be any number, positive or negative. Softmax turns the scores into probabilities: make every score positive with eˣ, then divide by the total so the shares add up to 1. Bigger score, bigger share, and the ordering never changes.

Math, decoded
pᵢ = e^(zᵢ) / Σⱼ e^(zⱼ) softmax: logits z → probabilities p L = −Σᵢ yᵢ · log(pᵢ) = −log(p_true) cross-entropy with a one-hot target y ∂L/∂z = p − y the gradient, again

With two classes this reduces exactly to the sigmoid and log loss of Section 10. The derivative of softmax on its own is a full Jacobian (each probability depends on every logit), but combined with cross-entropy it collapses to "predicted probabilities minus the one-hot truth".

Python
def softmax(z):
    e = np.exp(z - z.max(axis=-1, keepdims=True))   # subtracting the max changes nothing mathematically
    return e / e.sum(axis=-1, keepdims=True)

logits = np.array([2.0, 1.0, -1.0])      # raw scores for: buy, browse, leave

Numerical stability: never exponentiate raw logits

eˣ grows absurdly fast: e¹⁰⁰⁰ is far beyond what a computer can store, so a naive softmax on large logits returns nan. The fix uses a fact you can check by hand: subtracting the same number from every logit leaves the softmax unchanged (it cancels top and bottom). Subtract the largest logit first, and the biggest exponent is e⁰ = 1.

Python
big = np.array([1000.0, 999.0, 998.0])
with np.errstate(over="ignore", invalid="ignore"):
    naive = np.exp(big) / np.exp(big).sum()       # e^1000 overflows to inf
print("naive softmax :", naive)
print("stable softmax:", softmax(big).round(4))

def log_softmax(z):                                # the "log-sum-exp" trick
    m = z.max()
    return z - m - np.log(np.sum(np.exp(z - m)))
print("log-softmax   :", log_softmax(big).round(4))
Output
naive softmax : [nan nan nan]
stable softmax: [0.6652 0.2447 0.09  ]
log-softmax   : [-0.4076 -1.4076 -2.4076]

For the loss you need log(p), and computing softmax first and taking the log afterwards loses precision for tiny probabilities. The log-sum-exp form computes log-probabilities directly. This is why PyTorch's CrossEntropyLoss and BCEWithLogitsLoss expect raw logits, not probabilities: they combine the softmax (or sigmoid) and the log in one numerically safe step.

The training loop is the same as Section 10, with matrices instead of vectors. Every shopper's error row is p − y; the weight gradient is Xᵀ @ (P − Y) by Section 9's shape rule.

Linear and logistic models draw straight lines. Plenty of business patterns aren't straight: customers respond to an offer only in a middle band of age and income, not at either extreme. Here's such a pattern: class 1 sits inside a circle.

Intuition · blame flows backwards

A factory's product fails inspection. The inspector asks the last station how much its output contributed, then asks the station before that, and so on, multiplying sensitivities as she walks back up the line. Backpropagation is that walk. At each layer it does two things: work out the gradient for that layer's weights, and pass the blame further back through those same weights (a transpose, from Part 1).

Math, decoded · the four lines of backprop
dZ2 = p − y output error (Section 8's gift) dW2 = Hᵀ @ dZ2 gradient for layer-2 weights dH = dZ2 @ W2ᵀ send the error back through W2 dZ1 = dH ⊙ (1 − H²) through tanh: multiply by its local slope dW1 = Xᵀ @ dZ1 gradient for layer-1 weights

Every line is Part 1 (matrix multiplication, transpose, element-wise product) combined with Section 8 (multiply by the local slope). Deeper networks just repeat the middle pair once per extra layer.

Python
def init(hidden=8, seed=0):
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    return {"W1": r.normal(0, 1, (2, hidden)), "b1": np.zeros(hidden),
            "W2": r.normal(0, 1, (hidden, 1)) / np.sqrt(hidden), "b2": np.zeros(1)}

def forward(P, X):
    Z1 = X @ P["W1"] + P["b1"]          # (n, 8)
    H  = np.tanh(Z1)                    # hidden layer
    Z2 = H @ P["W2"] + P["b2"]          # (n, 1)
    p  = sigmoid(Z2).ravel()            # (n,)
    return Z1, H, p

def loss_fn(P, X, y):
    _, _, p = forward(P, X)
    p = np.clip(p, 1e-9, 1 - 1e-9)
    return -np.mean(y * np.log(p) + (1 - y) * np.log(1 - p))

def backward(P, X, y):
    Z1, H, p = forward(P, X)
    dZ2 = ((p - y) / len(y))[:, None]   # output layer: p − y again   (n, 1)
    dW2 = H.T @ dZ2                     # (8, 1)
    db2 = dZ2.sum(axis=0)
    dH  = dZ2 @ P["W2"].T               # send the error back through W2   (n, 8)
    dZ1 = dH * (1 - H ** 2)             # through tanh: slope is 1 − tanh²
    dW1 = X.T @ dZ1                     # (2, 8)
    db1 = dZ1.sum(axis=0)
    return {"W1": dW1, "b1": db1, "W2": dW2, "b2": db2}

Hand-written backprop is easy to get subtly wrong, so we check it against the nudge test from Section 2 on a few individual weights:

13Vanishing and exploding gradients

Backprop multiplies a local slope at every layer. Multiply many numbers together and two things can happen: if they're mostly below 1, the product shrinks towards zero; if they're mostly above 1, it blows up. Deep networks live or die by this.

Intuition · a photocopy of a photocopy

Photocopy a page, then copy the copy, and repeat twenty times. If each copy is a bit fainter, the twentieth is blank. If each copy is a bit darker, it's a black smudge. Each layer in backprop is one more photocopy of the error signal. The sigmoid's slope is at most 0.25, so with sigmoid layers every copy is fainter.

The multiplication chain

A simplified model: each layer multiplies the gradient by the same factor. Real networks vary layer to layer, but the effect is the same.

Here is a real experiment rather than a toy: build networks of increasing depth, run one backward pass, and measure how large the gradient is when it reaches the first layer.

Python
def first_layer_grad(depth, act, width=32, seed=0):
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    X = r.normal(0, 1, (64, width))
    Ws, Hs, Zs = [], [X], []
    for _ in range(depth):                                   # forward through `depth` layers
        scale = np.sqrt(2 / width) if act == "relu" else np.sqrt(1 / width)
        W = r.normal(0, scale, (width, width)); Ws.append(W)
        Z = Hs[-1] @ W; Zs.append(Z)
        Hs.append(np.maximum(0, Z) if act == "relu" else sigmoid(Z))
    dH = np.ones_like(Hs[-1]) / 64                           # pretend loss = mean of outputs
    for i in reversed(range(depth)):                         # backward, layer by layer
        local = (Zs[i] > 0).astype(float) if act == "relu" else Hs[i+1] * (1 - Hs[i+1])
        dZ = dH * local
        if i == 0:
            return np.linalg.norm(Hs[0].T @ dZ)              # size of gradient reaching layer 1
        dH = dZ @ Ws[i].T

for depth in [2, 5, 10, 20, 30]:
    print(f"depth {depth:2d}:  sigmoid → {first_layer_grad(depth, 'sigmoid'):.2e}   "
          f"relu → {first_layer_grad(depth, 'relu'):.2e}")

FixHow it helps

14Regularisation through the gradient: L2, L1 and weight decay

A model that fits its training data perfectly often fails on new data, because it has learned noise along with the pattern. Regularisation adds a penalty for large weights to the loss. Calculus shows exactly what each penalty does to every update, and why L1 and L2 behave so differently.

Intuition · a spring and a constant pull

An L2 penalty acts like a spring tied to zero: the further a weight strays, the harder it's pulled back, but the pull fades as it gets close, so it almost never reaches zero. An L1 penalty is a constant tug towards zero, the same size however small the weight is, so small, unhelpful weights get pulled all the way to exactly zero.

Math, decoded
L2 (Ridge): L_total = L + λ·Σ wᵢ² → gradient adds 2λ·wᵢ L1 (Lasso): L_total = L + λ·Σ |wᵢ| → gradient adds λ·sign(wᵢ) (not defined at exactly 0)

λ controls how strong the penalty is. The bias is usually left unpenalised. Because |w| has a corner at 0, L1 uses a "subgradient" there, and practical solvers handle it with special steps (coordinate descent or soft-thresholding).

Python
lam = 0.1
print("   w     L2 pull (2λw)   L1 pull (λ·sign w)")
for w in [2.0, 0.5, 0.1, 0.01]:
    print(f"{w:5}      {2 * lam * w:7.4f}          {lam * np.sign(w):6.3f}")
Output

  0.5       0.1000           0.100

The table is the whole story. As w shrinks from 2 to 0.01, L2's pull shrinks with it (0.4 down to 0.002), while L1's pull stays at 0.1. So L2 makes weights small, and L1 makes weights zero.

Weight decay: the same thing, written as an update

Plug the L2 gradient into the gradient-descent update and rearrange: w ← w − α(g + 2λw) = w·(1 − 2αλ) − αg. In words: shrink every weight by a small fixed fraction, then take the usual step. That is weight decay.

Python
w, g, lr, lam = np.array([1.5, -0.8]), np.array([0.2, 0.1]), 0.1, 0.05

step_penalty = w - lr * (g + 2 * lam * w)           # gradient of loss + λ‖w‖²
step_decay   = w * (1 - 2 * lr * lam) - lr * g      # "shrink the weights, then step"
print(step_penalty, step_decay, np.allclose(step_penalty, step_decay))
Output
[ 1.465 -0.802] [ 1.465 -0.802] True

For plain gradient descent the two forms are identical. For Adam they are not, because Adam rescales the gradient (penalty included) per weight. AdamW fixes this by applying the decay directly to the weights, outside the adaptive scaling, and it's the usual choice for training transformers.

In ML

Ridge and Lasso in scikit-learn (alpha is λ), Elastic Net (both penalties at once), weight_decay in every PyTorch optimiser, and the lambda and alpha parameters of XGBoost and LightGBM all come from this section. Lagrange multipliers (Section 7) show that "add a penalty λ‖w‖²" and "keep ‖w‖ below a limit" are two views of the same idea.

In business

Lasso's zeros are a practical gift: a churn model built on 200 candidate features that keeps only 15 is cheaper to compute, easier to explain to stakeholders, and needs fewer data pipelines to maintain in production. Ridge is the safer choice when many correlated features each carry a little signal, such as dozens of overlapping web-behaviour metrics.

15Mini-batches, momentum, RMSProp and Adam

Mini-batches: estimate the slope from a sample

Intuition · tasting a spoonful

To check whether a big pot of soup needs salt, you taste a spoonful, not the whole pot. The spoonful gives a slightly noisy but fast answer. Computing the gradient on all of the data for every step (batch gradient descent) is tasting the whole pot. Using one example (stochastic gradient descent) is tasting one drop. A mini-batch of 32 to 512 examples is the spoonful, and it's what almost everyone uses.

The mini-batch gradient points roughly downhill, with some noise. Much faster steps more than make up for the noise, and the noise even helps a little, nudging the model out of flat spots and shallow dips. One full pass over the data is called an epoch.

Smarter steps

Plain gradient descent struggled in the stretched valley of Section 4. Three classic upgrades target that problem:

OptimiserAnalogyIdea
MomentumA ball rolling downhill builds speedKeep a running average of past gradients. Consistent directions add up; zig-zags across the valley cancel out.
RMSPropA hiker who shortens her stride on steep, jumpy groundKeep a running average of each gradient's size, and divide by it. Steep directions get smaller steps, shallow directions bigger ones. Each weight gets its own effective learning rate.
AdamBoth of the aboveMomentum for direction plus RMSProp for step size, with a small correction for the first few steps.
Python
# A stretched valley: steep across (w2), shallow along (w1) — like unscaled features
def valley_loss(p): return 0.5 * (p[0] ** 2 + 25 * p[1] ** 2)
def valley_grad(p): return np.array([p[0], 25 * p[1]])

def sgd(p, g, s, lr):      return p - lr * g, s
def momentum(p, g, s, lr, beta=0.9):
    s["v"] = beta * s.get("v", 0) + g                      # running direction
    return p - lr * s["v"], s
def rmsprop(p, g, s, lr, beta=0.9, eps=1e-8):
    s["sq"] = beta * s.get("sq", 0) + (1 - beta) * g ** 2  # running size of each slope
    return p - lr * g / (np.sqrt(s["sq"]) + eps), s
def adam(p, g, s, lr, b1=0.9, b2=0.999, eps=1e-8):
    s["t"] = s.get("t", 0) + 1
    s["m"] = b1 * s.get("m", 0) + (1 - b1) * g             # momentum part
    s["v"] = b2 * s.get("v", 0) + (1 - b2) * g ** 2        # RMSProp part
    m_hat = s["m"] / (1 - b1 ** s["t"])                    # early-step bias correction
    v_hat = s["v"] / (1 - b2 ** s["t"])
    return p - lr * m_hat / (np.sqrt(v_hat) + eps), s

def steps_to_converge(opt, lr, start=(-4.0, 1.5), tol=1e-4, max_steps=5000):
    p, s = np.array(start), {}
    for t in range(1, max_steps + 1):
        p, s = opt(p, valley_grad(p), s, lr)
        if not np.all(np.isfinite(p)) or valley_loss(p) > 1e6:
            return None                                    # diverged
        if valley_loss(p) < tol:
            return t
    return None

# Fair comparison: give every optimiser its own best learning rate from the same grid
grid = [0.001, 0.003, 0.01, 0.03, 0.05, 0.07, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 1.0]
for name, opt in [("SGD", sgd), ("Momentum", momentum), ("RMSProp", rmsprop), ("Adam", adam)]:
    results = {lr: steps_to_converge(opt, lr) for lr in grid}
    ok = {lr: s for lr, s in results.items() if s}
    best_lr = min(ok, key=ok.get)
    print(f"{name:9s} best lr = {best_lr:<5}  steps = {ok[best_lr]:4d}   (diverged at {sum(v is None for v in results.values())} of {len(grid)} rates)")

16Learning-rate schedules

Intuition · parking a car

Pulling out of a parking space, you start slowly. On the open road you drive at full speed. Approaching your spot, you slow right down to place the car precisely. A learning-rate schedule does the same: a short warmup while the random starting weights produce wild gradients, full speed in the middle, then a gradual decay so the final steps can settle into the bottom of the valley instead of bouncing around it.

def warmup_cosine(step, total=1000, warmup=100, lr_max=1e-3, lr_min=1e-5):
    if step < warmup:
        return lr_max * (step + 1) / warmup                  # ramp up gently
    progress = (step - warmup) / (total - warmup)            # 0 → 1 after warmup
    return lr_min + 0.5 * (lr_max - lr_min) * (1 + np.cos(np.pi * progress))

for step in [0, 50, 99, 100, 300, 550, 800, 999]:

17How autograd works (what PyTorch does)

In Section 12 we wrote backprop by hand for one specific network. Frameworks do it automatically for any network you can write. The trick: make every number remember which operation produced it and how to pass a gradient back through that operation. Then backward() just walks the computational graph in reverse. Here's a complete working version in about 50 lines.

Python
import math

class Value:
    """A number that remembers how it was made, so it can send gradients back."""
    def __init__(self, data, parents=(), op=""):
        self.data, self.grad = data, 0.0
        self._parents, self._op = parents, op
        self._backward = lambda: None

    def __add__(self, other):
        other = other if isinstance(other, Value) else Value(other)
        out = Value(self.data + other.data, (self, other), "+")
        def _backward():                       # d(a+b)/da = 1, d(a+b)/db = 1
            self.grad += out.grad
            other.grad += out.grad
        out._backward = _backward
        return out

    def __mul__(self, other):
        other = other if isinstance(other, Value) else Value(other)
        out = Value(self.data * other.data, (self, other), "*")
        def _backward():                       # d(ab)/da = b, d(ab)/db = a
            self.grad += other.data * out.grad
            other.grad += self.data * out.grad
        out._backward = _backward
        return out

    def sigmoid(self):
        s = 1 / (1 + math.exp(-self.data))
        out = Value(s, (self,), "sigmoid")
        def _backward():
            self.grad += s * (1 - s) * out.grad
        out._backward = _backward
        return out

    def log(self):
        out = Value(math.log(self.data), (self,), "log")
        def _backward():
            self.grad += (1 / self.data) * out.grad
        out._backward = _backward
        return out

    def backward(self):
        order, seen = [], set()
        def visit(v):                          # topological order: parents before children
            if v not in seen:
                seen.add(v)
                for p in v._parents:
                    visit(p)
                order.append(v)
        visit(self)
        self.grad = 1.0                        # dL/dL = 1
        for v in reversed(order):              # walk the graph backwards
            v._backward()

Each operation stores a tiny _backward function containing its local slope rule from the table in Section 2. Now rebuild the one-neuron example from Section 8 and let the engine do the calculus:

Common mistakes and fixes

Mistakes and fixes
  1. Stepping with the gradient. w += lr * grad climbs uphill. The update subtracts.
  2. Loss becomes nan. Learning rate too high, unscaled features, or log(0). Lower the rate, scale inputs, clip probabilities before taking logs.
  3. Training is painfully slow. Features on very different scales (zig-zag valley). Standardise them.
  4. MSE for classification. Use cross-entropy; MSE + sigmoid gives weak gradients when confidently wrong.
  5. Trusting hand-written gradients. Run a gradient check against the nudge test.
  6. Deep sigmoid networks. Use ReLU-family activations with He initialisation, and residual connections for depth.
  7. Reading training accuracy as success. Always check on held-out data.
  8. Exponentiating raw logits. Subtract the max (log-sum-exp) and give frameworks logits, not probabilities.
  9. A gradient whose shape doesn't match its parameter. By the shape rule, that gradient is wrong. Fix the formula, not the transpose.
  10. Adding an L2 penalty to Adam and calling it weight decay. Use AdamW (decoupled weight decay) instead.

Where this leaves you

Part 1 gave you the language: data as vectors and matrices, predictions as dot products, layers as matrix multiplications. Part 2 gave you the engine: a loss to measure error, derivatives to find which way is downhill, the chain rule to push that information back through every layer, and update rules to take the step. The bicycle loop from the introduction (try, measure, adjust, repeat) is now something you can write in NumPy. One piece is still missing: what the model's outputs mean. Part 3 (probability) shows that the losses you minimised here are maximum likelihood in disguise and that regularisation is a prior, and it adds sampling and information theory.

Quick recap

  • Training = minimising a loss. The loss turns errors into one number, and it's a function of the weights, not the data.
  • The derivative is the slope; its sign says which way is uphill. Gradient descent steps against it: w ← w − α∇L, with α below 2 ÷ curvature.
  • With many weights, the gradient is the vector of partial derivatives. Scaling features turns a stretched valley into a round bowl and makes descent dramatically faster.
  • Sometimes you can solve ∇L = 0 directly (the normal equation). The Hessian tells a bowl from a saddle, and Lagrange multipliers handle constraints.
  • The chain rule multiplies local slopes along a graph; backprop applies it layer by layer with dW = Xᵀ @ dZ, and every gradient has its parameter's shape.
  • Sigmoid or softmax + cross-entropy gives the gradient p − y. Compute it from logits with the log-sum-exp trick.
  • Deep nets need ReLU, good initialisation, residual connections and clipping against vanishing and exploding gradients; L1/L2 penalties and AdamW's weight decay keep weights in check.
