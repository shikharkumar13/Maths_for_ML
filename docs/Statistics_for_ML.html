Your new churn model scores 87% accuracy. The old one scored 86%. Should you replace it? Most people say yes. A statistician asks three questions first: 87% on how many customers? Would a different test set give 84% or 90%? Did any information from the test set sneak into training? This article teaches you to ask those questions, and to answer them with a few lines of code.

Part 3 was about probability: if you know how the world works, what data should you expect? Statistics runs the other way: you have some data, so what can you honestly say about the world? Every number a model produces (an accuracy, an AUC, a lift in sales) comes from a limited sample, so every one of them is a guess with a margin of error. Ignoring that margin is one of the most common ways ML projects go wrong, and one of the easiest to avoid.

As before, everything is built from everyday examples (orders, customers, test sets, A/B tests), with pictures you can play with and code you can run.

By the end you will be able to:

  • explain what an estimator is, and what bias and variance mean for it,
  • describe data properly before modelling: skew, medians, logs and the right kind of correlation,
  • put an honest error bar on almost any model metric, using a formula or the bootstrap,
  • explain the bias–variance trade-off, and recognise underfitting and overfitting,
  • set up train/validation/test splits and cross-validation without leaking information,
  • compare two models fairly, read an A/B test, and detect when production data has drifted.

Three golden rules for this article

1. Every metric is an estimate. "87% accuracy" really means "87%, give or take something". Always ask for the "something".

2. The test set is sealed until the very end. Anything that looks at test data before the final check (choosing features, tuning, scaling) makes the result too optimistic.

3. Compare like with like. Two models must be judged on the same data, the same way. Otherwise you're measuring the data, not the models.

1Populations, samples and estimators

Intuition · tasting soup

A cook tastes one spoonful to judge a whole pot of soup. The pot is the population; the spoonful is the sample; "it needs salt" is an estimate. A good spoonful comes from a well-stirred pot (a random sample). A spoonful skimmed from the top, where the oil floats, would mislead the cook every time.

An estimator is a recipe that turns a sample into a guess: "add up the order values and divide by n" is an estimator of the average order value. Two questions decide whether a recipe is good:

Intuition · the dartboard

Imagine throwing many darts, one per possible sample. Bias is whether the darts are centred on the bullseye or off to one side. Variance is how widely they scatter. You want both small: darts that cluster tightly around the bullseye. A tight cluster in the wrong place (low variance, high bias) can be just as bad as a loose cluster around the right place (high variance, low bias).

Math, decoded
bias(θ̂) = (average of θ̂ over many samples) − true value 0 means "unbiased" variance(θ̂) = how much θ̂ changes from sample to sample
Python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
population = rng.lognormal(mean=7, sigma=0.6, size=1_000_000)   # every order ever: values in ₹
true_mean, true_var = population.mean(), population.var()

samples = rng.choice(population, size=(20_000, 5))   # 20,000 small samples, 5 orders each

print(f"true mean            : ₹{true_mean:,.0f}")
print(f"average sample mean  : ₹{samples.mean(axis=1).mean():,.0f}   ← unbiased: hits the truth on average")
print(f"true variance        : {true_var:,.0f}")
print(f"average var (÷ n)    : {samples.var(axis=1, ddof=0).mean():,.0f}   ← biased: too small")
print(f"average var (÷ n − 1): {samples.var(axis=1, ddof=1).mean():,.0f}   ← unbiased")
Output
true mean            : ₹1,314
average sample mean  : ₹1,314   ← unbiased: hits the truth on average
true variance        : 748,778
average var (÷ n)    : 604,682   ← biased: too small
average var (÷ n − 1): 755,853   ← unbiased

Small samples of 5 orders each: the sample mean is right on average (unbiased), but the variance computed by dividing by n comes out about 20% too small. Dividing by n − 1 fixes that. Why? The sample mean is pulled towards the sample's own points, so distances to it look a little smaller than distances to the true mean. Dividing by a slightly smaller number compensates. (This makes the variance unbiased; its square root, the standard deviation, is still a touch too small on average, but the difference rarely matters.) This is why np.std has a ddof argument, and why pandas' .std() (which uses n − 1) and NumPy's (which uses n by default) give slightly different answers.

Draw many samples, watch the estimates

Order values are skewed (a few big orders). Choose a sample size and an estimator, then draw 500 samples. The histogram shows where the 500 estimates land; the dashed line is the true value.

Two things to notice: bigger samples make the histogram narrower (variance shrinks like 1 ÷ n, so the spread shrinks like 1 ÷ √n, as in Part 3), and the sample median of skewed data sits below the mean, because it estimates a different thing: the typical order, not the average one.

In ML

A model's test accuracy is an estimator of its accuracy on all future data. Cross-validation reduces that estimator's variance by averaging over several splits; the bootstrap and confidence intervals measure it. And the "bias–variance trade-off" in Section 5 is the same dartboard idea, applied to whole models instead of single numbers.

In business

A survey of customers who chose to reply, or a dataset of only the users who completed sign-up, is a spoonful from the top of the pot: sampling bias. Collecting more data the same way doesn't fix it, and models trained on it inherit it. Ask "how was this sample collected?" before "how big is it?".

Check yourself

1. An estimator's guesses average out to exactly the truth, but vary a lot. Biased or unbiased? High or low variance? Answer: unbiased, high variance.

2. You survey only customers who contacted support. What's the problem? Answer: sampling bias; they are not representative of all customers.

2Describing data before you model it

Before training anything, look at your data's shape. Five minutes here prevents days of confusion later.

Mean, median and skew

Intuition · a billionaire walks into a café

Ten people in a café earn ₹50,000 a month each. A billionaire walks in. The mean income in the room jumps many times over; the median (the middle person) is still ₹50,000. The mean is pulled by extremes; the median isn't. Data with a long tail of big values is called right-skewed, and money (incomes, order values, house prices) is almost always like this.

Python
from scipy.stats import skew

print(f"mean order   : ₹{population.mean():,.0f}")
print(f"median order : ₹{np.median(population):,.0f}")
print(f"share of orders below the mean: {np.mean(population < population.mean()):.1%}")
print(f"skewness, raw values : {skew(population):.2f}")
print(f"skewness, log values : {skew(np.log(population)):.2f}")
Output
mean order   : ₹1,314
median order : ₹1,097
share of orders below the mean: 61.8%
skewness, raw values : 2.26
skewness, log values : -0.00

62% of orders are below the "average" order, so the average is not typical. Taking the log of the values removes the skew completely here (skewness from 2.26 to 0). Linear models and distance-based methods often work better on log-transformed money features, and errors on a log scale treat a ₹100 miss on a ₹500 order like a ₹1,000 miss on a ₹5,000 order.

Correlation, the right way

Math, decoded
  • Pearson's r measures how well the points fit a straight line, from −1 to +1. It's easily dragged around by a single outlier.
  • Spearman's ρ replaces values by their ranks (1st, 2nd, 3rd…) and then computes Pearson on the ranks. It measures any steadily rising or falling relationship and is much less affected by outliers (though not immune to them).
Python
from scipy.stats import pearsonr, spearmanr

x = rng.normal(size=50)                    # e.g. minutes on site
y = rng.normal(size=50)                    # e.g. something unrelated
print(f"unrelated         : Pearson {pearsonr(x, y)[0]:+.2f}   Spearman {spearmanr(x, y)[0]:+.2f}")

x_out, y_out = np.append(x, 15), np.append(y, 15)          # add ONE extreme customer
print(f"+ one outlier     : Pearson {pearsonr(x_out, y_out)[0]:+.2f}   Spearman {spearmanr(x_out, y_out)[0]:+.2f}")

curve = np.exp(x)                                           # perfectly rising, but curved
print(f"curved, monotonic : Pearson {pearsonr(x, curve)[0]:+.2f}   Spearman {spearmanr(x, curve)[0]:+.2f}")
Output
unrelated         : Pearson +0.25   Spearman +0.22
+ one outlier     : Pearson +0.87   Spearman +0.26
curved, monotonic : Pearson +0.87   Spearman +1.00

Three lessons in three lines. First, with only 50 points, two completely unrelated variables can show a correlation of 0.2 or more just by chance (here +0.25). Second, one extreme customer turned that weak noise into an impressive-looking 0.87 for Pearson, while Spearman stayed near 0.26. Third, a perfectly consistent but curved relationship gets Spearman's full +1.00 while Pearson says only 0.87.

Pearson vs Spearman

Set the true relationship, then add an outlier or bend the pattern. Watch which correlation tells the truth.

Common mistake · correlation is not causation

Ice-cream sales and sunburn cases rise together, but ice cream doesn't cause sunburn; hot weather drives both. In business data, a hidden common cause is everywhere: customers who use the premium feature churn less, but maybe it's because loyal customers are the ones who try premium features. A model can use a correlation to predict; deciding what to change needs an experiment (Section 10).

In ML

Exploring data drives feature engineering: log-transform skewed features, use medians for robust imputation of missing values, check correlations between features (multicollinearity, Part 1) and with the target. Tree-based models are unaffected by skew in their input features (a log doesn't change where a split falls); linear models, neural networks and k-NN often benefit from fixing it.

Check yourself

1. House prices: mean ₹95 lakh, median ₹60 lakh. Which way is the data skewed? Answer: right-skewed; a few very expensive houses pull the mean up.

2. Your scatter plot has one point far away from the others. Which correlation should you trust more? Answer: Spearman's, and also look at the point: is it an error?

3How sure is a test score? The standard error

Intuition · a cricket batter's average

A batter who scores 80 in her first two innings doesn't have a "true" average of 80; a couple of good days can do that. After 100 innings, her average means something. A model's test accuracy works the same way: on 100 test customers it's a rough guess; on 10,000 it's solid.

Math, decoded
accuracy is a proportion: SE = √( p × (1 − p) / n ) rough 95% range: p ± 1.96 × SE (the bell curve from Part 3)

Each test example is a Bernoulli trial (right or wrong, Part 3), so the accuracy is the average of n yes/no values, and the σ ÷ √n rule applies (as long as the test examples are independent, e.g. not several rows from the same customer).

Python
true_acc = 0.85                           # the model's real accuracy on ALL future customers

for n in [100, 500, 2_000, 10_000]:
    se = np.sqrt(true_acc * (1 - true_acc) / n)               # standard error of an accuracy
    sims = rng.binomial(n, true_acc, size=10_000) / n          # 10,000 possible test sets
    lo, hi = np.percentile(sims, [2.5, 97.5])
    print(f"test set of {n:>6,}: ±{1.96 * se:.3f}   (simulated 95% range {lo:.3f} to {hi:.3f})")
Output
test set of    100: ±0.070   (simulated 95% range 0.780 to 0.920)
test set of    500: ±0.031   (simulated 95% range 0.818 to 0.880)
test set of  2,000: ±0.016   (simulated 95% range 0.835 to 0.866)
test set of 10,000: ±0.007   (simulated 95% range 0.843 to 0.857)

With 100 test customers, a model whose true accuracy is 85% could easily score anywhere from 78% to 92%. That's why "87% vs 86%" on a small test set tells you almost nothing. To pin accuracy down to about ±1 point, you need several thousand test examples. The formula and the 10,000 simulated test sets agree.

How big should the test set be?

Set the model's true accuracy and the test-set size. The bars show how likely each score is, across all the different test sets of that size you might have drawn.

In business

A dashboard that shows "model accuracy this week: 91.2%" with no error bar invites over-reaction to noise. A drop to 89.8% on a week with 300 cases is probably nothing; on a week with 30,000 cases it's almost certainly a real drop. Showing the range alongside the number saves a lot of unnecessary meetings.

Check yourself

1. Accuracy 0.9 on 400 test examples. Roughly what is the 95% range? Answer: SE = √(0.9 × 0.1 / 400) = 0.015, so about 0.87 to 0.93.

2. To halve the error bar, how much bigger must the test set be? Answer: four times bigger.

4Confidence intervals and the bootstrap

The formula in Section 3 works for accuracy. But what about AUC, F1, median revenue per customer, or "precision at the top 100"? Their formulas are messy or don't exist. The bootstrap gives an error bar for almost any metric with one simple trick. (It struggles with very small samples and with extremes such as "the maximum", but works well for everyday model metrics.)

Intuition · pretend your sample is the population

You can't go back and collect new test sets. So make pretend ones: draw rows from your test set at random, with replacement (some rows get picked twice, some not at all) until you have a new set of the same size. Compute the metric. Repeat a couple of thousand times. The spread of those results shows how much the metric could have wobbled.

What a 95% confidence interval really means

"If we repeated the whole process (collect data, compute the interval) many times, about 95% of the intervals would contain the true value." It does not mean "there's a 95% chance the true value is in this particular interval", though in everyday use people often read it that way. The practical takeaway is the same: values inside the interval are plausible; values far outside it aren't.

Python
from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score

n = 400
y_test = (rng.random(n) < 0.3).astype(int)                 # 30% of test customers churned
scores = y_test * 1.0 + rng.normal(0, 0.8, n)              # a model's churn scores

auc = roc_auc_score(y_test, scores)
boot = []
for _ in range(2_000):
    idx = rng.integers(0, n, n)                             # resample the test set WITH replacement
    if y_test[idx].min() != y_test[idx].max():               # need both classes present
        boot.append(roc_auc_score(y_test[idx], scores[idx]))
lo, hi = np.percentile(boot, [2.5, 97.5])
print(f"AUC = {auc:.3f}   95% bootstrap interval: {lo:.3f} to {hi:.3f}")
Output
AUC = 0.833   95% bootstrap interval: 0.792 to 0.872

An AUC of 0.833 on 400 customers really means "somewhere around 0.79 to 0.87". If a competing model scores 0.85 on the same data, you haven't shown it's better yet (Section 8 shows how to check properly).

In ML

Report every final metric with an interval: "AUC 0.833 (95% CI 0.79–0.87)". The bootstrap also powers bagging and random forests, which train each tree on a bootstrap sample of the rows and average the trees to cut variance. It's also used to put error bars on feature importances and on predictions themselves.

In business

"The new pricing raised revenue per customer by ₹42 (95% CI ₹5 to ₹80)" tells a manager both the likely gain and the risk that it's close to zero. A bare "₹42" invites over-confidence. The bootstrap lets analysts give that range for medians and other metrics where textbook formulas don't apply.

Check yourself

1. In a bootstrap sample of size n, why do some rows appear twice? Answer: rows are drawn with replacement.

2. Two models' intervals overlap a lot. Can you conclude they're equally good? Answer: no. Overlap doesn't prove they're equal, and two intervals can even overlap a little while the difference is real. Test the difference itself, ideally with a paired comparison on the same rows (Section 8).

5The bias–variance trade-off

This is one of the most important statistical ideas in machine learning. It explains why a model that's perfect on training data can be useless on new data, and why the "best" model is usually neither the simplest nor the most complex.

Intuition · three students and an exam

Student A only learned one rule of thumb, "the answer is usually C". He does badly on practice tests and the real exam alike: underfitting (high bias). Student B memorised every practice question word for word. She aces the practice tests and fails the real exam, whose questions are slightly different: overfitting (high variance). Student C learned the underlying ideas. Decent on practice, decent on the exam: a good fit.

Math, decoded
expected test error = bias² + variance + noise
  • Bias²: how far the model's average prediction (over many possible training sets) is from the truth. Too-simple models have high bias.
  • Variance: how much the model's prediction changes when you train it on a different sample. Too-flexible models have high variance.
  • Noise: randomness in the data itself. No model can beat it.

This exact split holds for squared error; the same idea guides model choice for every loss.

To see it, fit polynomials of different degrees to many noisy samples of the same seasonal pattern, and measure each part directly:

Python
import warnings
warnings.simplefilter("ignore", np.exceptions.RankWarning)

def true_pattern(x):
    return np.sin(2 * np.pi * x)                           # e.g. demand through the year

x_grid = np.linspace(0, 1, 100)

x_train = np.linspace(0, 1, 15)                         # 15 measurements, evenly spread

def experiment(degree, n_datasets=300, noise=0.3):
    preds, train_err, test_err = [], [], []
    for _ in range(n_datasets):                             # many different "years" of noisy data
        x = x_train
        y = true_pattern(x) + rng.normal(0, noise, len(x))
        coefs = np.polyfit(x, y, degree)                    # fit a polynomial of this degree
        preds.append(np.polyval(coefs, x_grid))
        train_err.append(np.mean((np.polyval(coefs, x) - y) ** 2))
        y_new = true_pattern(x_grid) + rng.normal(0, noise, 100)
        test_err.append(np.mean((np.polyval(coefs, x_grid) - y_new) ** 2))
    preds = np.array(preds)
    bias2 = np.mean((preds.mean(axis=0) - true_pattern(x_grid)) ** 2)
    variance = np.mean(preds.var(axis=0))
    return bias2, variance, np.mean(train_err), np.mean(test_err)

print("degree   bias²   variance   train error   test error")
for d in [1, 3, 5, 9, 12]:
    b2, v, tr, te = experiment(d)
    print(f"{d:>6}   {b2:5.3f}   {v:8.3f}   {tr:11.3f}   {te:10.3f}")
print("noise² (the floor no model can beat) =", 0.3 ** 2)
Output
degree   bias²   variance   train error   test error
     1   0.211      0.010         0.323        0.312
     3   0.006      0.022         0.072        0.117
     5   0.000      0.032         0.051        0.122
     9   0.000      0.066         0.030        0.156
    12   0.000      0.517         0.012        0.609
noise² (the floor no model can beat) = 0.09

Degree 1 (a straight line) can't bend to follow the seasons: huge bias, tiny variance, poor on both training and test data. Degree 3 to 5 captures the pattern: bias near zero and modest variance, giving the lowest test error (about 0.12, against a noise floor of 0.09 that no model can beat). Degree 12 hugs every noisy training point (training error 0.012!) but swings wildly between them: tiny bias, big variance, and the worst test error.

Too simple, just right, too flexible

Each thin line is the same type of model trained on a different noisy sample. The thick line is their average; the dashed line is the true pattern. Right: training and test error for every degree.

In ML

Almost every model setting is a bias–variance dial: tree depth, number of neighbours in k-NN, polynomial degree, network size, the regularisation strength λ, the number of boosting rounds. Ensembles attack one side directly: bagging and random forests average many high-variance models to cut variance; boosting adds models one after another to cut bias. (Very large neural networks can behave in more surprising ways, sometimes called "double descent", but the train-vs-validation diagnosis above still applies.)

In business

An overfit model looks brilliant in the presentation (it was evaluated on data it had seen) and disappoints in production. Asking "what was the validation error, and how far is it from training error?" is the single best question a manager can ask a data team.

Check yourself

1. Training accuracy 99%, validation accuracy 71%. Bias or variance problem? Answer: variance (overfitting).

2. Training and validation accuracy are both 62%, far below what you need. What should you try? Answer: a more flexible model or better features; the model is underfitting.

6Train, validation, test and data leakage

Intuition · practice papers, mock exam, real exam

A student learns from practice papers (the training set), checks progress and adjusts their study plan using a mock exam (the validation set), and is graded once on the real exam (the test set). If the student sees the real exam questions beforehand, the grade stops meaning anything, however good it looks.

SetUsed forTypical share
TrainingFitting the model's weights60–80%
ValidationChoosing features, models and settings (or use cross-validation, Section 7)10–20%
TestOne final, honest estimate. Never used for decisions.10–20%

Data leakage is when information that wouldn't be available at prediction time, or information from the evaluation data, sneaks into training. It makes results look far better than they are. Here's a dramatic example: 100 customers, 5,000 features of pure random noise, and random labels. There is nothing to learn.

Python
from sklearn.feature_selection import SelectKBest, f_classif
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline

X_noise = rng.normal(size=(100, 5_000))     # 100 customers, 5,000 random features
y_noise = rng.integers(0, 2, 100)          # random labels: there is NOTHING to learn

# Wrong: pick the 20 "best" features using ALL the labels, then cross-validate
X_picked = SelectKBest(f_classif, k=20).fit_transform(X_noise, y_noise)
leaky = cross_val_score(LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000), X_picked, y_noise, cv=5).mean()

# Right: feature selection happens INSIDE each training fold
pipe = make_pipeline(SelectKBest(f_classif, k=20), LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000))
honest = cross_val_score(pipe, X_noise, y_noise, cv=5).mean()

print(f"leaky accuracy : {leaky:.1%}   ← looks like a great model")
print(f"honest accuracy: {honest:.1%}   ← the truth: no better than a coin flip")
Output
leaky accuracy : 89.0%   ← looks like a great model
honest accuracy: 50.0%   ← the truth: no better than a coin flip

The leaky version chose the 20 features that happened to line up with the labels across all 100 customers, including the ones later used for validation. Cross-validation then rewarded it for a pattern it had already peeked at. The honest pipeline does the selection inside each training fold and reveals the truth. This exact mistake has shown up in published research.

The common kinds of leakage
  • Preprocessing on all the data: fitting a scaler, imputer, feature selector or PCA before splitting. Fix: put every step in a Pipeline so it's fitted on training folds only.
  • Target leakage: a feature that is only known after the outcome, such as "number of cancellation calls" in a churn model, or "account closed date". Fix: ask "would I know this value at the moment I make the prediction?".
  • Time leakage: shuffling time-ordered data, so the model trains on the future to predict the past. Fix: split by time (Section 7).
  • Duplicate or related rows: the same customer, patient or image appearing in both train and test. Fix: split by group (all rows of a customer go to the same side).
  • Tuning on the test set: checking test scores again and again while adjusting the model. Fix: tune on validation data; touch the test set once.
In ML

One of the main jobs of scikit-learn's Pipeline and ColumnTransformer is preventing leakage: inside cross-validation, every step is fitted on the training split and only applied to the validation split. In deep learning, the same rule applies to normalisation statistics, vocabularies and tokenisers built from data.

In business

Leakage is one of the most common reasons a model that "scored 95% in testing" fails in production. A churn model built with "complaints in the 30 days before the customer left" used a window that ends at the churn date. In production you predict weeks earlier, when those complaints haven't happened yet, so the model is far weaker than its test score. A leakage review should be part of every model sign-off.

Check yourself

1. You standardise all features using the mean and std of the full dataset, then split. Is that leakage? Answer: yes, a mild form; fit the scaler on the training data only.

2. A loan-default model uses "number of missed payments" from the loan's whole history. What's wrong? Answer: target leakage; missed payments happen after the approval decision.

7Cross-validation

A single validation split gives a single, wobbly number. Cross-validation uses every row for validation exactly once, and averages.

Intuition · rotating the judges

A talent show splits its audience into 5 groups. Each round, one group votes and the other four watch. After 5 rounds every group has voted once, and the average vote is far more reliable than any single group's. In k-fold cross-validation, the data is split into k groups (folds); each fold takes a turn as the validation set while the model trains on the rest.

How the folds rotate

Each square is a row of data, in time order from left to right. Step through the folds. Switch to time-series mode to see why shuffled folds are wrong for time-ordered data.

Python
from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split, StratifiedKFold
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True)            # 569 patients, 30 features

    X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.2, random_state=seed, stratify=y)
    single.append(model.fit(X_tr, y_tr).score(X_te, y_te))
print("five single splits:", np.round(single, 3))

print("5-fold CV scores  :", folds.round(3))
print(f"CV estimate       : {folds.mean():.3f} ± {folds.std():.3f}")

Five different random splits gave five different accuracies, from 97.4% to 99.1%: the same model, just different luck. The fold scores themselves range from 95.6% to 100%.

In ML

GridSearchCV and RandomizedSearchCV run cross-validation for every setting they try. If you also want an honest estimate of the tuned model, either keep a separate test set or use nested cross-validation (an outer loop for evaluation, an inner loop for tuning). Deep-learning projects usually use one validation split instead, because training k times is expensive.

In business

For forecasting (demand, revenue, staffing), always validate the way the model will be used: train on everything up to a date and forecast the weeks after it. A forecast model validated with shuffled folds will look much better than it ever performs.

Check yourself

1. With 5-fold CV, how many times is the model trained, and how often is each row used for validation? Answer: 5 times; each row is validated exactly once.

2. Your data has 10 rows per customer. Which splitter should you use? Answer: GroupKFold (grouped by customer).

8Comparing models fairly

Compare on the same rows: paired comparison

Intuition · two runners on the same track

To see which of two runners is faster, you don't time one on a flat road and the other uphill. You race them on the same track, on the same day. Comparing models works the same way: score both on the same test rows and look at the difference row by row. Most rows are easy (both models get them right) and cancel out; the comparison then focuses on the few rows where the models disagree.

Python
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier

X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.3, random_state=1, stratify=y)
pred_a = model.fit(X_tr, y_tr).predict(X_te)                                   # model A
pred_b = DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=0).fit(X_tr, y_tr).predict(X_te) # model B

right_a, right_b = (pred_a == y_te), (pred_b == y_te)
print(f"accuracy A = {right_a.mean():.3f}   accuracy B = {right_b.mean():.3f}")

diffs = []
for _ in range(5_000):                                 # paired bootstrap: same rows for both models
    idx = rng.integers(0, len(y_te), len(y_te))
    diffs.append(right_a[idx].mean() - right_b[idx].mean())
lo, hi = np.percentile(diffs, [2.5, 97.5])
print(f"A − B = {right_a.mean() - right_b.mean():+.3f}   95% interval {lo:+.3f} to {hi:+.3f}")
print(f"test rows where they disagree: {(pred_a != pred_b).sum()} of {len(y_te)}")
Output
accuracy A = 0.959   accuracy B = 0.947
A − B = +0.012   95% interval -0.018 to +0.041
test rows where they disagree: 6 of 171

Model A looks 1.2 points better, but the paired bootstrap interval runs from −1.8 to +4.1 points, which includes zero. The two models disagree on only 6 of 171 patients, so there just isn't enough evidence yet. The honest conclusion: "no clear winner on this test set". You would need more test data, or repeated cross-validation, to separate them.

The winner's curse: trying many things

Intuition · a room full of coin-flippers

Ask 1,000 people to each flip a coin 10 times. Almost certainly, someone will get 9 or 10 heads (each person has about a 1% chance, and there are 1,000 of them). They aren't skilled; you just looked at a lot of people. Trying many models, feature sets or settings and keeping the one with the best test score has the same effect: the best score is partly luck, so it overestimates how good the winner really is.

Python
n_test = 200
y_hidden = rng.integers(0, 2, n_test)                  # labels nobody can predict

best = []
for n_models in [1, 10, 100, 1_000]:
    accs = [(rng.integers(0, 2, n_test) == y_hidden).mean() for _ in range(n_models)]   # random guessers
    best.append(max(accs))
    print(f"try {n_models:>5,} useless models → best test accuracy {max(accs):.1%}")
Output
try     1 useless models → best test accuracy 47.5%
try    10 useless models → best test accuracy 54.5%
try   100 useless models → best test accuracy 59.0%
try 1,000 useless models → best test accuracy 60.0%

Every one of those "models" is a random guesser with a true accuracy of 50%. Yet the best of 1,000 scored 60% on the test set. This is why the test set must be used once, at the end: once you start choosing based on test scores, the test score stops being an honest estimate.

How lucky can a useless model look?

Every model here is a coin-flipper (true accuracy 50%). Choose how many you try and how big the test set is, then see the best score.

In ML

This is why public leaderboards (Kaggle's public vs private scores) often reshuffle at the end, why heavy hyperparameter searches need a held-out test set, and why "we tried 200 features and kept the significant ones" produces fragile models. The fix is always the same: decide on validation data, confirm once on untouched test data, and prefer simpler choices when differences are within the error bars.

In business

If a team tested 30 campaign variants and reports the best one's uplift, expect the real uplift to be smaller when it's rolled out. Ask how many variants were tried, and re-test the winner on fresh traffic before scaling it.

Check yourself

1. Why is a paired comparison more sensitive than comparing two separate intervals? Answer: it cancels out the rows both models get right (or wrong) and focuses on the differences.

2. You tried 50 model settings and the best scored 91% on the test set. Is 91% an honest estimate? Answer: no, it's optimistic; you chose it because it scored well on that very set.

9Baselines and imbalanced data

Intuition · the weather forecaster who always says "sunny"

In a city where it's sunny 95% of the time, a forecaster who always says "sunny" is right 95% of the time, and completely useless on the one day you need an umbrella. A model's score only means something next to a baseline: what you'd get with no model at all.

Python
from sklearn.dummy import DummyClassifier
from sklearn.metrics import accuracy_score, balanced_accuracy_score, recall_score

y_fraud = (rng.random(10_000) < 0.05).astype(int)     # 5% of transactions are fraud
X_any = rng.normal(size=(10_000, 3))

always_no = DummyClassifier(strategy="most_frequent").fit(X_any, y_fraud)
pred = always_no.predict(X_any)
print(f"accuracy          : {accuracy_score(y_fraud, pred):.1%}   ← sounds great")
print(f"balanced accuracy : {balanced_accuracy_score(y_fraud, pred):.1%}")
print(f"frauds caught     : {recall_score(y_fraud, pred):.1%}   ← catches none")
Output
accuracy          : 94.7%   ← sounds great
balanced accuracy : 50.0%
frauds caught     : 0.0%   ← catches none

A model that does nothing gets 94.7% accuracy because only about 5% of transactions are fraud. Balanced accuracy (the average of the accuracy on each class) drops to 50%, the coin-flip level, and recall shows it catches zero frauds. Always compute the baseline first; scikit-learn's DummyClassifier and DummyRegressor do it in one line.

Better choices when one class is rare
  • Metrics: precision, recall, F1, PR-AUC and balanced accuracy, rather than plain accuracy. (Part 3's Bayes section shows why precision collapses when positives are rare.)
  • Splits: stratified, so every fold has some positives.
  • Training: class weights (class_weight="balanced"), resampling, or simply a different decision threshold than 0.5.
  • Error bars: with 30 frauds in the test set, recall can only move in steps of 1/30 ≈ 3.3 points, and its interval is wide. Count your positives, not just your rows.
In business

Always present a model as "compared with what we do today": the current rules, a simple heuristic, or doing nothing. "Our model catches 70% of fraud at a 2% review rate, versus 35% for the current rules" is a business case. "Our model has 97% accuracy" isn't.

Check yourself

1. 2% of customers churn. What accuracy does "predict nobody churns" get? Answer: 98%.

2. Name two metrics that would expose that useless model. Answer: recall (0%) and balanced accuracy (50%); also F1 or PR-AUC.

10A/B tests: did the new model really help?

Offline metrics only predict business impact. The real test is an A/B test: show the old model (A) to one random half of users and the new model (B) to the other half, then compare an outcome such as conversion. Random assignment is what makes it an experiment: it breaks hidden common causes (Section 2), so a difference can be credited to the model.

Intuition · the "innocent until proven guilty" rule

A court starts by assuming the defendant is innocent, and convicts only if the evidence would be very surprising for an innocent person. A hypothesis test starts by assuming the new model makes no difference (the null hypothesis H₀), and only declares a winner if the observed difference would be very surprising if H₀ were true.

Math, decoded · the p-value, in one sentence

"If there were truly no difference, how often would random chance alone produce a gap at least as big as the one we saw?" A small p-value (below α, usually 0.05) means the result would be unusual if there were no real difference, so "it was just luck" becomes hard to believe. It is not the probability that the new model is better, and it says nothing about whether the difference is big enough to matter.

z = (p_B − p_A) / SE how many standard errors apart the two conversion rates are |z| ≈ 1.96 ↔ p-value ≈ 0.05
Python
from scipy.stats import norm

# Old recommendation model (A) vs new model (B), shown to random halves of visitors
buys_a, visits_a = 480, 10_000
buys_b, visits_b = 540, 10_000

p_a, p_b = buys_a / visits_a, buys_b / visits_b
p_pool = (buys_a + buys_b) / (visits_a + visits_b)
se_pool = np.sqrt(p_pool * (1 - p_pool) * (1 / visits_a + 1 / visits_b))
z = (p_b - p_a) / se_pool
p_value = 2 * (1 - norm.cdf(abs(z)))                   # two-sided

se_diff = np.sqrt(p_a * (1 - p_a) / visits_a + p_b * (1 - p_b) / visits_b)
lo, hi = (p_b - p_a) - 1.96 * se_diff, (p_b - p_a) + 1.96 * se_diff
print(f"conversion A = {p_a:.2%}   B = {p_b:.2%}   lift = {100 * (p_b - p_a):+.2f} percentage points")
print(f"z = {z:.2f}   p-value = {p_value:.3f}")
print(f"95% interval for the lift: {100 * lo:+.2f} to {100 * hi:+.2f} percentage points")
Output
conversion A = 4.80%   B = 5.40%   lift = +0.60 percentage points
z = 1.93   p-value = 0.054
95% interval for the lift: -0.01 to +1.21 percentage points

The new model lifted conversion from 4.80% to 5.40%. That's a lift of 0.6 percentage points (a 12.5% relative increase). Encouraging, but the p-value is 0.054 and the interval for the lift runs from about −0.01 to +1.21 percentage points: it just includes zero. Honest summary: "promising, but not conclusive at the usual 5% level". If the test hasn't yet reached the sample size you planned, let it run to that size; if it has, don't keep extending it until p dips below 0.05 (that's the peeking mistake below). Run a larger, planned follow-up instead. The interval is more useful than the yes/no verdict, because it shows the plausible size of the gain.

Plan the sample size before you start

Small lifts need surprisingly large tests. This rough formula gives the visitors needed per group to detect a lift with 80% probability (called the test's power):

Python
def visitors_needed(p_base, lift, alpha=0.05, power=0.80):
    """Rough visitors per group to detect a lift (two-sided test)."""
    z_a, z_b = norm.ppf(1 - alpha / 2), norm.ppf(power)
    p_new = p_base + lift
    var = p_base * (1 - p_base) + p_new * (1 - p_new)
    return int(np.ceil((z_a + z_b) ** 2 * var / lift ** 2))

for lift in [0.002, 0.005, 0.01]:
    print(f"detect 4.8% → {0.048 + lift:.1%}: about {visitors_needed(0.048, lift):,} visitors per group")
Output
detect 4.8% → 5.0%: about 182,872 visitors per group
detect 4.8% → 5.3%: about 30,105 visitors per group
detect 4.8% → 5.8%: about 7,875 visitors per group

Common A/B mistakes
  • Peeking: checking every day and stopping the moment p < 0.05 inflates false wins badly. Decide the sample size in advance (or use methods built for continuous monitoring).
  • Many metrics, one winner: test 20 metrics where nothing really changed and, on average, one will look "significant" by chance at α = 0.05. Pick the main metric before the test.
  • Statistical vs practical significance: with millions of users, a lift of 0.01 percentage points can be "significant" and still not worth the engineering cost. Look at the interval and the money.
In ML

Online experiments are the final judge of recommendation, ranking, pricing and fraud models, because offline metrics (AUC, NDCG) don't always move business metrics. Many teams run a shadow mode first (the new model predicts but doesn't act), then a small A/B test, then a gradual rollout.

Check yourself

1. p-value = 0.003. Does that mean there's a 99.7% chance model B is better? Answer: no. It means a gap this large would be rare (0.3%) if there were truly no difference.

2. To detect a lift half as big, roughly how many more visitors do you need? Answer: about four times as many.

11Is production still like training? Detecting drift

Intuition · a map of a city that keeps growing

A map drawn five years ago is still mostly right, but new roads and flyovers make it slowly wrong. A model is a map of the data it was trained on. When customers, prices or behaviour change, the model's map drifts out of date, often silently: it keeps giving confident answers, just worse ones.

Kind of driftWhat changedExample
Data drift (covariate shift)The inputs' distributionA campaign brings in older customers than before
Concept driftThe link between inputs and the targetAfter a competitor's price cut, the same customers now churn more
Label driftHow common each outcome isFraud rate doubles during the festive season
Two standard drift checks
  • Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) statistic: the largest vertical gap between the two cumulative distributions (training vs production), from 0 (identical) to 1 (completely different). scipy.stats.ks_2samp also gives a p-value.
  • Population Stability Index (PSI): bins the feature using training percentiles and compares the shares in each bin (on the binned shares it equals KL divergence from Part 3 measured in both directions and added together). A common industry rule of thumb: below 0.1 stable, 0.1–0.25 worth watching, above 0.25 significant shift.
Python
from scipy.stats import ks_2samp

train_age = rng.normal(35, 8, 5_000)                  # customer age when the model was trained
prod_same = rng.normal(35, 8, 5_000)                  # production, nothing changed
prod_drift = rng.normal(40, 10, 5_000)                # production after a campaign brought older customers

def psi(expected, actual, bins=10):
    edges = np.percentile(expected, np.linspace(0, 100, bins + 1))
    edges[0], edges[-1] = -np.inf, np.inf
    e = np.histogram(expected, edges)[0] / len(expected)
    a = np.histogram(actual, edges)[0] / len(actual)
    e, a = np.clip(e, 1e-6, None), np.clip(a, 1e-6, None)
    return np.sum((a - e) * np.log(a / e))

for name, prod in [("no change", prod_same), ("after the campaign", prod_drift)]:
    ks = ks_2samp(train_age, prod)
    print(f"{name:19s} KS = {ks.statistic:.3f} (p = {ks.pvalue:.3g})   PSI = {psi(train_age, prod):.3f}")
Output
no change           KS = 0.022 (p = 0.163)   PSI = 0.007
after the campaign  KS = 0.247 (p = 1.72e-134)   PSI = 0.334

With no change, KS is tiny and PSI is 0.007: stable. After the campaign, PSI is 0.33, well above the 0.25 alarm level. One practical warning: with thousands of rows, KS p-values become tiny even for harmless shifts, so teams usually alert on the size of the change (the KS statistic or PSI), not on the p-value.

In ML

Drift monitoring is a core MLOps job: track the input distributions, the prediction distribution and (when labels arrive) the real performance, and trigger alerts or retraining when they move. Tools such as Evidently and many cloud ML platforms compute KS, PSI and similar scores per feature on a schedule.

In business

Drift checks turn "the model seems worse lately" into a dated, measurable alert, often before revenue is hit. They also explain surprises: a sudden jump in loan approvals may be a shift in applicants after a marketing push, not a broken model. Deciding in advance what level of drift triggers a review or a retrain is a simple, high-value policy.

Check yourself

1. A feature's PSI between training and last month is 0.31. What should you do? Answer: investigate; it's a significant shift, and the model may need retraining or a fix upstream.

2. Inputs look the same as in training, but accuracy has dropped. What kind of drift is that? Answer: most likely concept drift (the relationship between inputs and outcome changed), once you've ruled out a broken data pipeline or labelling change.

Common mistakes and fixes

Mistakes and fixes
  1. Reporting a metric without an error bar. Add a formula-based or bootstrap interval.
  2. Preprocessing before splitting. Put every step inside a Pipeline.
  3. Using the test set to choose anything. Choose on validation or CV; test once.
  4. Shuffling time-series data. Use time-based splits.

Quick recap

  • Every metric is an estimate. An estimator has bias (off-centre) and variance (scatter); good ones are low on both. Divide by n − 1 for an unbiased variance.
  • Look before you model: money is skewed, so medians and logs help; Spearman resists outliers; correlation is not causation.
  • A test score wobbles by about √(p(1 − p)/n). The bootstrap gives an interval for almost any metric.
  • Bias–variance: too simple underfits, too flexible overfits. Compare training and validation error to tell which.
  • Seal the test set. Do all preprocessing inside pipelines, split by time or group when needed, and cross-validate to choose.
  • Compare fairly: paired, on the same rows, against a baseline, remembering that the best of many tries is partly luck.
  • After launch, confirm with an A/B test (plan the sample size; read the interval, not just the p-value) and watch for drift with KS and PSI.
